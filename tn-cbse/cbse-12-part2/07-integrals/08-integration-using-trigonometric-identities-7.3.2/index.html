7.3.2 Integration using trigonometric identities

Rewrite powers and products of sines and cosines as sums — then integrate term by term

In This Section

  • Using trigonometric identities to turn awkward integrands into standard ones
  • Lowering powers: \( \cos^2 x \) and \( \sin^3 x \)
  • Turning products such as \( \sin 2x\cos 3x \) into sums
  • Why two different-looking answers can both be correct

Identities to the Rescue

When the integrand involves some trigonometric functions, we use some known identities to find the integral as illustrated through the following example.

Identities used below
\( \cos 2x = 2\cos^2 x - 1 \)\( \Rightarrow\ \cos^2 x = \dfrac{1 + \cos 2x}{2} \)
\( \sin x\cos y = \dfrac{1}{2}\big[\sin(x + y) + \sin(x - y)\big] \)product → sum
\( \sin 3x = 3\sin x - 4\sin^3 x \)\( \Rightarrow\ \sin^3 x = \dfrac{3\sin x - \sin 3x}{4} \)
Example 7

Find (i) \( \int \cos^2 x\,dx \)     (ii) \( \int \sin 2x\cos 3x\,dx \)     (iii) \( \int \sin^3 x\,dx \)

Solution

(i) Recall the identity \( \cos 2x = 2\cos^2 x - 1 \), which gives

\[ \cos^2 x = \frac{1 + \cos 2x}{2} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Therefore,}\quad \int \cos^2 x\,dx &= \frac{1}{2}\int (1 + \cos 2x)\,dx = \frac{1}{2}\int dx + \frac{1}{2}\int \cos 2x\,dx \\ &= \frac{x}{2} + \frac{1}{4}\sin 2x + \text{C} \end{aligned} \]

(ii) Recall the identity \( \sin x\cos y = \dfrac{1}{2}\big[\sin(x + y) + \sin(x - y)\big] \)   (Why?)

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Then}\quad \int \sin 2x\cos 3x\,dx &= \frac{1}{2}\left[\int \sin 5x\,dx - \int \sin x\,dx\right] \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\left[-\frac{1}{5}\cos 5x + \cos x\right] + \text{C} \\ &= -\frac{1}{10}\cos 5x + \frac{1}{2}\cos x + \text{C} \end{aligned} \]

(iii) From the identity \( \sin 3x = 3\sin x - 4\sin^3 x \), we find that

\[ \sin^3 x = \frac{3\sin x - \sin 3x}{4} \] \[ \begin{aligned} \text{Therefore,}\quad \int \sin^3 x\,dx &= \frac{3}{4}\int \sin x\,dx - \frac{1}{4}\int \sin 3x\,dx \\ &= -\frac{3}{4}\cos x + \frac{1}{12}\cos 3x + \text{C} \end{aligned} \]

Alternatively, \( \displaystyle\int \sin^3 x\,dx = \int \sin^2 x\sin x\,dx = \int (1 - \cos^2 x)\sin x\,dx \)

Put \( \cos x = t \) so that \( -\sin x\,dx = dt \).

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{Therefore,}\quad \int \sin^3 x\,dx &= -\int \left(1 - t^2\right)dt = -\int dt + \int t^2\,dt = -t + \frac{t^3}{3} + \text{C} \\ &= -\cos x + \frac{1}{3}\cos^3 x + \text{C} \end{aligned} \]
Remark

It can be shown using trigonometric identities that both answers are equivalent.
