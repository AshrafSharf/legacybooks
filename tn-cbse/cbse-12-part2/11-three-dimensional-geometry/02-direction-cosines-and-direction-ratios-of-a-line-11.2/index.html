11.2 Direction Cosines and Direction Ratios of a Line

Three cosines that pin down the direction of any line in space — and the numbers proportional to them

In This Section

  • Direction angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) and direction cosines \(\cos\alpha, \cos\beta, \cos\gamma\) of a directed line
  • Why a line has two sets of direction cosines, and how a directed line gives a unique set \(l, m, n\)
  • Direction ratios \(a, b, c\): any three numbers proportional to \(l, m, n\)
  • Recovering direction cosines from direction ratios using \(l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1\)

Direction Angles and Direction Cosines

From Chapter 10, recall that if a directed line L passing through the origin makes angles \(\alpha\), \(\beta\) and \(\gamma\) with \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\)-axes, respectively, called direction angles, then cosine of these angles, namely, \(\cos\alpha\), \(\cos\beta\) and \(\cos\gamma\) are called direction cosines of the directed line L.

Definition — Direction Cosines

If a directed line L through the origin makes angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\) with the positive \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\)-axes, then \[ \cos\alpha,\quad \cos\beta,\quad \cos\gamma \] are the direction cosines of L.

If we reverse the direction of L, then the direction angles are replaced by their supplements, i.e., \(\pi - \alpha\), \(\pi - \beta\) and \(\pi - \gamma\). Thus, the signs of the direction cosines are reversed.

Note that a given line in space can be extended in two opposite directions and so it has two sets of direction cosines. In order to have a unique set of direction cosines for a given line in space, we must take the given line as a directed line. These unique direction cosines are denoted by \(l\), \(m\) and \(n\).

Remark

If the given line in space does not pass through the origin, then, in order to find its direction cosines, we draw a line through the origin and parallel to the given line. Now take one of the directed lines from the origin and find its direction cosines as two parallel line have same set of direction cosines.

Direction Ratios

Definition — Direction Ratios

Any three numbers which are proportional to the direction cosines of a line are called the direction ratios of the line. If \(l, m, n\) are direction cosines and \(a, b, c\) are direction ratios of a line, then \[ a = \lambda l,\quad b = \lambda m \quad\text{and}\quad c = \lambda n, \quad\text{for any nonzero } \lambda \in \mathbf{R}. \]

Note

Some authors also call direction ratios as direction numbers.

From direction ratios to direction cosines

Let \(a, b, c\) be direction ratios of a line and let \(l, m\) and \(n\) be the direction cosines (d.c’s) of the line. Then

\[ \frac{l}{a} = \frac{m}{b} = \frac{n}{c} = k \ \text{(say)}, \quad k \text{ being a constant.} \]
1

Therefore

\[ l = ak,\quad m = bk,\quad n = ck \tag{1} \]
2

But \(l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1\). Therefore

\[ k^2\left(a^2 + b^2 + c^2\right) = 1 \quad\text{or}\quad k = \pm\frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} \]
3

Hence, from (1), the d.c.’s of the line are

Result — Direction cosines from direction ratios
\[ l = \pm\frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}},\quad m = \pm\frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}},\quad n = \pm\frac{c}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}} \]

where, depending on the desired sign of \(k\), either a positive or a negative sign is to be taken for \(l\), \(m\) and \(n\).

For any line, if \(a, b, c\) are direction ratios of a line, then \(ka, kb, kc\); \(k \ne 0\) is also a set of direction ratios. So, any two sets of direction ratios of a line are also proportional. Also, for any line there are infinitely many sets of direction ratios.

Key Insight

Direction cosines are unique (up to the choice of direction) because they satisfy \(l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1\); direction ratios carry the same “direction” information but have no fixed size.
