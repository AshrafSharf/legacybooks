9.4.1 Differential equations with variables separable

Put all the y's on one side, all the x's on the other, and integrate

In This Section

  • When a first order–first degree equation \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \mathrm{F}(x, y)\) is of variable separable type
  • The method: separate the variables, then integrate both sides
  • General solutions (Examples 4, 5) and particular solutions (Example 6)
  • Finding curves from slope conditions (Examples 7, 8)
  • An application to continuously compounded interest (Example 9)

The Method of Separating Variables

A first order-first degree differential equation is of the form

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \mathrm{F}(x, y) \tag{1} \]
Definition — Variable Separable Type

If \(\mathrm{F}(x, y)\) can be expressed as a product \(g(x)\,h(y)\), where, \(g(x)\) is a function of \(x\) and \(h(y)\) is a function of \(y\), then the differential equation (1) is said to be of variable separable type. The differential equation (1) then has the form

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = h(y)\cdot g(x) \tag{2} \]
1

If \(h(y) \neq 0\), separating the variables, (2) can be rewritten as

\[ \frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy = g(x)\,dx \tag{3} \]
2

Integrating both sides of (3), we get

\[ \int \frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy = \int g(x)\,dx \tag{4} \]
Result

Thus, (4) provides the solutions of given differential equation in the form

\[ \mathrm{H}(y) = \mathrm{G}(x) + \mathrm{C} \]

Here, \(\mathrm{H}(y)\) and \(\mathrm{G}(x)\) are the anti derivatives of \(\dfrac{1}{h(y)}\) and \(g(x)\) respectively and C is the arbitrary constant.

General Solutions

Example 4

Find the general solution of the differential equation \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{x + 1}{2 - y}\), \((y \neq 2)\).

Solution We have

\[ \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x + 1}{2 - y} \tag{1} \]

Separating the variables in equation (1), we get

\[ (2 - y)\,dy = (x + 1)\,dx \tag{2} \]

Integrating both sides of equation (2), we get

\[ \begin{aligned} \int (2 - y)\,dy &= \int (x + 1)\,dx \\ \text{or}\quad 2y - \frac{y^2}{2} &= \frac{x^2}{2} + x + \mathrm{C}_1 \\ \text{or}\quad x^2 + y^2 + 2x - 4y + 2\,\mathrm{C}_1 &= 0 \\ \text{or}\quad x^2 + y^2 + 2x - 4y + \mathrm{C} &= 0, \quad \text{where } \mathrm{C} = 2\mathrm{C}_1 \end{aligned} \]

which is the general solution of equation (1).

Example 5

Find the general solution of the differential equation \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{1 + y^2}{1 + x^2}\).

Solution Since \(1 + y^2 \neq 0\), therefore separating the variables, the given differential equation can be written as

\[ \frac{dy}{1 + y^2} = \frac{dx}{1 + x^2} \tag{1} \]

Integrating both sides of equation (1), we get

\[ \begin{aligned} \int \frac{dy}{1 + y^2} &= \int \frac{dx}{1 + x^2} \\ \text{or}\quad \tan^{-1} y &= \tan^{-1} x + \mathrm{C} \end{aligned} \]

which is the general solution of equation (1).

Particular Solutions

Example 6

Find the particular solution of the differential equation \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -4xy^2\) given that \(y = 1\), when \(x = 0\).

Solution If \(y \neq 0\), the given differential equation can be written as

\[ \frac{dy}{y^2} = -4x\,dx \tag{1} \]

Integrating both sides of equation (1), we get

\[ \begin{aligned} \int \frac{dy}{y^2} &= -4\int x\,dx \\ \text{or}\quad -\frac{1}{y} &= -2x^2 + \mathrm{C} \end{aligned} \] \[ \text{or}\quad y = \frac{1}{2x^2 - \mathrm{C}} \tag{2} \]

Substituting \(y = 1\) and \(x = 0\) in equation (2), we get, \(\mathrm{C} = -1\).

Now substituting the value of C in equation (2), we get the particular solution of the given differential equation as \(y = \dfrac{1}{2x^2 + 1}\).

Example 7

Find the equation of the curve passing through the point \((1, 1)\) whose differential equation is \(x\,dy = (2x^2 + 1)\,dx\) \((x \neq 0)\).

Solution The given differential equation can be expressed as

\[ dy^{*} = \left(\frac{2x^2 + 1}{x}\right)dx^{*} \] \[ \text{or}\quad dy = \left(2x + \frac{1}{x}\right)dx \tag{1} \]

Integrating both sides of equation (1), we get

\[ \int dy = \int \left(2x + \frac{1}{x}\right)dx \] \[ \text{or}\quad y = x^2 + \log|x| + \mathrm{C} \tag{2} \]

Equation (2) represents the family of solution curves of the given differential equation but we are interested in finding the equation of a particular member of the family which passes through the point \((1, 1)\). Therefore substituting \(x = 1, y = 1\) in equation (2), we get \(\mathrm{C} = 0\).

Now substituting the value of C in equation (2) we get the equation of the required curve as \(y = x^2 + \log|x|\).

Note (*)

The notation \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\) due to Leibnitz is extremely flexible and useful in many calculation and formal transformations, where, we can deal with symbols \(dy\) and \(dx\) exactly as if they were ordinary numbers. By treating \(dx\) and \(dy\) like separate entities, we can give neater expressions to many calculations.

Refer: Introduction to Calculus and Analysis, volume-I page 172, By Richard Courant, Fritz John Spinger – Verlog New York.

Example 8

Find the equation of a curve passing through the point \((-2, 3)\), given that the slope of the tangent to the curve at any point \((x, y)\) is \(\dfrac{2x}{y^2}\).

Solution We know that the slope of the tangent to a curve is given by \(\dfrac{dy}{dx}\).

\[ \text{so,}\quad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2x}{y^2} \tag{1} \]

Separating the variables, equation (1) can be written as

\[ y^2\,dy = 2x\,dx \tag{2} \]

Integrating both sides of equation (2), we get

\[ \int y^2\,dy = \int 2x\,dx \] \[ \text{or}\quad \frac{y^3}{3} = x^2 + \mathrm{C} \tag{3} \]

Substituting \(x = -2, y = 3\) in equation (3), we get \(\mathrm{C} = 5\).

Substituting the value of C in equation (3), we get the equation of the required curve as

\[ \frac{y^3}{3} = x^2 + 5 \quad \text{or} \quad y = (3x^2 + 15)^{\frac{1}{3}} \]

An Application: Continuous Growth

Example 9

In a bank, principal increases continuously at the rate of 5% per year. In how many years Rs 1000 double itself?

Solution Let P be the principal at any time \(t\). According to the given problem,

\[ \frac{dp}{dt} = \left(\frac{5}{100}\right)\times \mathrm{P} \] \[ \text{or}\quad \frac{dp}{dt} = \frac{\mathrm{P}}{20} \tag{1} \]

separating the variables in equation (1), we get

\[ \frac{dp}{\mathrm{P}} = \frac{dt}{20} \tag{2} \]

Integrating both sides of equation (2), we get

\[ \begin{aligned} \log \mathrm{P} &= \frac{t}{20} + \mathrm{C}_1 \\ \text{or}\quad \mathrm{P} &= e^{\frac{t}{20}}\cdot e^{\mathrm{C}_1} \end{aligned} \] \[ \text{or}\quad \mathrm{P} = \mathrm{C}\,e^{\frac{t}{20}} \quad (\text{where } e^{\mathrm{C}_1} = \mathrm{C}) \tag{3} \]

Now \(\mathrm{P} = 1000\), when \(t = 0\).

Substituting the values of P and \(t\) in (3), we get \(\mathrm{C} = 1000\). Therefore, equation (3), gives

\[ \mathrm{P} = 1000\,e^{\frac{t}{20}} \]

Let \(t\) years be the time required to double the principal. Then

\[ 2000 = 1000\,e^{\frac{t}{20}} \ \Rightarrow\ t = 20\log_e 2 \]
