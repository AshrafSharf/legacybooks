Chapter 9

Quadratic Equations

Mastering second-degree equations through factoring, completing the square, and the quadratic formula

What You'll Learn

Quadratic equations are a natural progression from the linear equations you already know. In this chapter, you'll learn multiple methods to solve equations where the variable is raised to the second power, understand how their solutions behave, and see how they connect to parabolic curves.

  • Write quadratic equations in standard form \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\)
  • Solve quadratic equations by factoring and the zero product property
  • Solve by completing the square and understand perfect square trinomials
  • Derive and apply the quadratic formula
  • Use the discriminant to determine the nature of roots
  • Graph parabolas and find vertex, axis of symmetry, and intercepts
  • Apply the sum and product of roots relationships
  • Solve real-world word problems involving quadratic equations

1. What Is a Quadratic Equation?

Definition — Quadratic Equation

A quadratic equation in one variable is an equation in which the highest power of the unknown is the second power. Its standard form is:

$$ax^2 + bx + c = 0 \qquad (a \neq 0)$$

where \(a\) is the coefficient of \(x^2\) (the quadratic term), \(b\) is the coefficient of \(x\) (the linear term), and \(c\) is the constant term. All three are real numbers.

For example, in the equation \(x^2 - 3x + 2 = 0\), we identify:

$$a = 1, \quad b = -3, \quad c = 2$$

Quadratic equations are divided into two classes:

  • Complete quadratic equations: all three terms are present, e.g., \(x^2 - 3x + 2 = 0\)
  • Incomplete quadratic equations: either \(b = 0\) (missing the linear term, e.g., \(x^2 - 49 = 0\)) or \(c = 0\) (missing the constant, e.g., \(x^2 - 6x = 0\))
Key Insight

Unlike linear equations which have exactly one solution, a quadratic equation can have two distinct real solutions, one repeated real solution, or no real solutions (complex roots). This is one of the most important differences between first- and second-degree equations.

Identifying a, b, and c

Before applying any solution method, always rewrite the equation in standard form \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) and identify the coefficients. Here are several examples:

Equation (standard form)\(a\)\(b\)\(c\)
\(x^2 - 3x + 2 = 0\)1\(-3\)2
\(x^2 - 49 = 0\)10\(-49\)
\(3x^2 + 4x - 7 = 0\)34\(-7\)
\(2x^2 + 5x = 0\)250
Common Mistake

An equation may not look quadratic at first glance. For example, \(x = 4 - \tfrac{3}{x}\) does not appear quadratic, but multiplying through by \(x\) gives \(x^2 - 4x + 3 = 0\). Always clear fractions, remove parentheses, and collect like terms before identifying the equation type.

2. Solving by Factoring

The first and often simplest method for solving a quadratic equation is factoring. This method relies on a fundamental property of real numbers.

Zero Product Property

If \(A \cdot B = 0\), then \(A = 0\) or \(B = 0\) (or both).

In words: if a product of factors equals zero, at least one factor must be zero.

The Factoring Method — Step by Step

  1. Write the quadratic equation in standard form: \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\).
  2. Factor the left side into two first-degree (linear) factors.
  3. Set each factor containing the variable equal to zero.
  4. Solve each resulting linear equation.
  5. Check each root by substituting back into the original equation.
Worked Example — Solving by Factoring

Solve: \(x^2 + 4x = 21\)

Step 1: Rewrite in standard form: \(x^2 + 4x - 21 = 0\)

Step 2: Factor: \((x + 7)(x - 3) = 0\)

Step 3: Set each factor to zero:

$$x + 7 = 0 \qquad \text{or} \qquad x - 3 = 0$$

Step 4: Solve:

$$x = -7 \qquad \text{or} \qquad x = 3$$

Step 5: Check:

$$(-7)^2 + 4(-7) = 49 - 28 = 21 \;\checkmark$$ $$(3)^2 + 4(3) = 9 + 12 = 21 \;\checkmark$$

Solving Incomplete Quadratics by Factoring

When \(c = 0\), the equation has the form \(ax^2 + bx = 0\). Factor out \(x\):

$$x(ax + b) = 0 \implies x = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad x = -\frac{b}{a}$$
Key Insight

One root of any equation of the form \(ax^2 + bx = 0\) is always \(x = 0\). Don't forget it! The other root is \(x = -b/a\).

Extraction of Roots

When \(b = 0\), the equation has the form \(ax^2 = c\). Solve by isolating \(x^2\) and taking square roots:

$$x^2 = \frac{c}{a} \implies x = \pm\sqrt{\frac{c}{a}}$$

If \(c/a\) is negative, there are no real roots.

Common Mistake

When taking square roots, always include both the positive and negative roots. Writing \(x = \sqrt{9} = 3\) and forgetting \(x = -3\) means losing half the solution!

3. Solving by Completing the Square

Not all quadratic trinomials can be factored neatly. Completing the square is a powerful technique that works for every quadratic equation, whether factorable or not.

Definition — Perfect Square Trinomial

A trinomial of the form \(x^2 + 2ax + a^2\) is a perfect square trinomial because it factors as \((x + a)^2\). Both the first and third terms must be perfect squares, and the middle term must equal twice the product of their square roots.

$$(x + a)^2 = x^2 + 2ax + a^2$$

The Method — Step by Step

  1. Move the constant \(c\) to the right side of the equation.
  2. If the coefficient of \(x^2\) is not 1, divide every term by that coefficient.
  3. Take half the coefficient of \(x\), square it, and add this value to both sides.
  4. Write the left side as a perfect square: \(\bigl(x + \tfrac{b}{2}\bigr)^2\).
  5. Take the square root of both sides (remembering the \(\pm\)).
  6. Solve the two resulting linear equations.
  7. Check both roots in the original equation.
The Core Identity
$$x^2 + bx + \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 = \left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2$$

Add \(\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2\) to both sides to "complete" the left side into a perfect square.

Worked Example — Completing the Square

Solve: \(x^2 + 6x - 7 = 0\)

Step 1: Move constant: \(x^2 + 6x = 7\)

Step 2: Coefficient of \(x^2\) is already 1.

Step 3: Half of 6 is 3; \(3^2 = 9\). Add 9 to both sides:

$$x^2 + 6x + 9 = 7 + 9 = 16$$

Step 4: Factor: \((x + 3)^2 = 16\)

Step 5: Take square roots: \(x + 3 = \pm 4\)

Step 6: Solve:

$$x + 3 = 4 \implies x = 1 \qquad\qquad x + 3 = -4 \implies x = -7$$

Step 7: Check: \(1^2 + 6(1) - 7 = 0\;\checkmark\) and \((-7)^2 + 6(-7) - 7 = 0\;\checkmark\)

When the Roots Are Irrational

Completing the square shines when factoring fails. Consider \(x^2 + 2x - 5 = 0\):

$$x^2 + 2x = 5$$ $$x^2 + 2x + 1 = 6$$ $$(x + 1)^2 = 6$$ $$x + 1 = \pm\sqrt{6}$$ $$x = -1 + \sqrt{6} \quad \text{or} \quad x = -1 - \sqrt{6}$$

These irrational solutions are perfectly valid real numbers (approximately \(1.449\) and \(-3.449\)).

4. The Quadratic Formula

By applying the method of completing the square to the general quadratic equation \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\), we obtain the most powerful tool for solving any quadratic equation.

The Quadratic Formula
$$x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$

This formula gives both solutions of any quadratic equation \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) directly from the coefficients \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\).

Derivation (Completing the Square on the General Form)

Starting from \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\):

$$ax^2 + bx = -c$$ $$x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x = -\frac{c}{a} \qquad\text{(divide by }a\text{)}$$ $$x^2 + \frac{b}{a}x + \frac{b^2}{4a^2} = \frac{b^2}{4a^2} - \frac{c}{a} \qquad\text{(complete the square)}$$ $$\left(x + \frac{b}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{b^2 - 4ac}{4a^2}$$ $$x + \frac{b}{2a} = \pm\frac{\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$$ $$\boxed{x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}}$$
Worked Example — Quadratic Formula

Solve: \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\)

Identify: \(a = 1,\; b = -5,\; c = 6\)

$$x = \frac{-(-5) \pm \sqrt{(-5)^2 - 4(1)(6)}}{2(1)} = \frac{5 \pm \sqrt{25 - 24}}{2} = \frac{5 \pm 1}{2}$$ $$x = \frac{5 + 1}{2} = 3 \qquad \text{or} \qquad x = \frac{5 - 1}{2} = 2$$
Key Insight — Which Method to Use?
  • Factoring: Fastest when the equation factors neatly with integer roots.
  • Completing the square: Good for understanding the structure; essential for deriving the quadratic formula and for converting to vertex form.
  • Quadratic formula: Works for every quadratic equation, no guessing required. When in doubt, use this.

5. The Discriminant

The expression under the square root in the quadratic formula tells us everything about the nature of the roots, even before we compute them.

Definition — The Discriminant

The discriminant of the quadratic equation \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) is the quantity:

$$\Delta = b^2 - 4ac$$
✔✔

\(\Delta > 0\)

Two distinct real roots

The parabola crosses the x-axis at two points.

✔

\(\Delta = 0\)

One repeated real root

The parabola is tangent to the x-axis (touches at one point).

✘

\(\Delta < 0\)

No real roots

The parabola does not intersect the x-axis. (Roots are complex.)

6. Graphing Parabolas

Every quadratic equation \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\) produces a U-shaped curve called a parabola. The real roots of \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) are the x-values where this parabola crosses the x-axis.

Key Properties of \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\)
  • Vertex (turning point): \(\displaystyle x = -\frac{b}{2a}\), then compute \(y\) at that \(x\).
  • Axis of symmetry: the vertical line \(\displaystyle x = -\frac{b}{2a}\).
  • Direction: opens upward if \(a > 0\); opens downward if \(a < 0\).
  • y-intercept: set \(x = 0\); the y-intercept is \(c\).
  • x-intercepts (roots): set \(y = 0\) and solve \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\).
Key Insight — Reading Roots from a Graph

The roots of \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\) are precisely the x-coordinates where the parabola \(y = ax^2 + bx + c\) intersects the x-axis (where \(y = 0\)). If the curve just touches the axis, there is one repeated root. If it never crosses, there are no real roots.

7. Sum and Product of Roots

If \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) are the two roots of \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\), then the quadratic can be written as \(a(x - r_1)(x - r_2) = 0\). Expanding and comparing coefficients reveals two elegant relationships.

Vieta's Formulas for Quadratics
$$r_1 + r_2 = -\frac{b}{a} \qquad\qquad r_1 \cdot r_2 = \frac{c}{a}$$

The sum of the roots equals \(-b/a\), and the product of the roots equals \(c/a\).

Quick Verification

For \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\), the roots are \(x = 2\) and \(x = 3\).

  • Sum: \(2 + 3 = 5 = -(-5)/1 = -b/a\) ✓
  • Product: \(2 \times 3 = 6 = 6/1 = c/a\) ✓
Key Insight

These relationships are useful as a quick check. After solving a quadratic, verify that your two roots add up to \(-b/a\) and multiply to \(c/a\). If they don't, you've made an arithmetic error somewhere!

8. Word Problems with Quadratics

Many real-world situations lead naturally to quadratic equations: area and geometry problems, projectile motion, optimization, and more. The key is translating the words into an equation in standard form, then solving with any of the methods you've learned.

Worked Example — Area Problem

Problem: A rectangle has an area of 48 square units. Its length is 2 more than its width. Find the dimensions.

Setup: Let \(w\) = width. Then length = \(w + 2\).

$$w(w + 2) = 48$$ $$w^2 + 2w - 48 = 0$$

Solve by factoring:

$$(w + 8)(w - 6) = 0$$ $$w = -8 \quad \text{or} \quad w = 6$$

Since width must be positive, \(w = 6\) and length = \(6 + 2 = 8\).

Check: \(6 \times 8 = 48\;\checkmark\)

Common Mistake

In word problems, always check whether both mathematical solutions make physical sense. A negative width or a negative time in a projectile problem should be rejected, even though it is a valid algebraic root.

Worked Problems

Problem 1 — Solve by Factoring

Solve \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\).

Solution

We need two numbers that multiply to \(6\) and add to \(-5\). Those are \(-2\) and \(-3\).

$$x^2 - 5x + 6 = (x - 2)(x - 3) = 0$$

Setting each factor to zero:

$$x - 2 = 0 \implies x = 2 \qquad \text{or} \qquad x - 3 = 0 \implies x = 3$$

Check:

$$2^2 - 5(2) + 6 = 4 - 10 + 6 = 0 \;\checkmark$$ $$3^2 - 5(3) + 6 = 9 - 15 + 6 = 0 \;\checkmark$$

Roots: \(x = 2\) and \(x = 3\)

Problem 2 — Completing the Square

Solve \(x^2 + 6x + 2 = 0\) by completing the square.

Solution

Step 1: Move the constant: \(x^2 + 6x = -2\)

Step 2: Half of 6 is 3; \(3^2 = 9\). Add 9 to both sides:

$$x^2 + 6x + 9 = -2 + 9 = 7$$

Step 3: Factor the left side:

$$(x + 3)^2 = 7$$

Step 4: Take square roots:

$$x + 3 = \pm\sqrt{7}$$ $$x = -3 \pm \sqrt{7}$$

Check: Substituting \(x = -3 + \sqrt{7}\):

$$(-3+\sqrt{7})^2 + 6(-3+\sqrt{7}) + 2 = 9 - 6\sqrt{7} + 7 - 18 + 6\sqrt{7} + 2 = 0 \;\checkmark$$

Roots: \(x = -3 + \sqrt{7} \approx -0.354\) and \(x = -3 - \sqrt{7} \approx -5.646\)

Problem 3 — Quadratic Formula

Solve \(2x^2 - 3x - 5 = 0\) using the quadratic formula.

Solution

Identify: \(a = 2,\; b = -3,\; c = -5\)

$$x = \frac{-(-3) \pm \sqrt{(-3)^2 - 4(2)(-5)}}{2(2)}$$ $$x = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{9 + 40}}{4} = \frac{3 \pm \sqrt{49}}{4} = \frac{3 \pm 7}{4}$$ $$x = \frac{3 + 7}{4} = \frac{10}{4} = \frac{5}{2} \qquad \text{or} \qquad x = \frac{3 - 7}{4} = \frac{-4}{4} = -1$$

Check:

$$2\!\left(\tfrac{5}{2}\right)^2 - 3\!\left(\tfrac{5}{2}\right) - 5 = 2\cdot\tfrac{25}{4} - \tfrac{15}{2} - 5 = \tfrac{25}{2} - \tfrac{15}{2} - 5 = 5 - 5 = 0 \;\checkmark$$ $$2(-1)^2 - 3(-1) - 5 = 2 + 3 - 5 = 0 \;\checkmark$$

Roots: \(x = \frac{5}{2}\) and \(x = -1\)

Problem 4 — Using the Discriminant

Without solving, determine the nature of the roots of \(3x^2 + 2x + 5 = 0\).

Solution

Identify: \(a = 3,\; b = 2,\; c = 5\)

Compute the discriminant:

$$\Delta = b^2 - 4ac = (2)^2 - 4(3)(5) = 4 - 60 = -56$$

Since \(\Delta = -56 < 0\), the equation has no real solutions. The parabola \(y = 3x^2 + 2x + 5\) lies entirely above the x-axis and never crosses it.

Problem 5 — Finding the Vertex

Find the vertex of the parabola \(y = x^2 - 4x + 7\).

Solution

The x-coordinate of the vertex is:

$$x = -\frac{b}{2a} = -\frac{-4}{2(1)} = \frac{4}{2} = 2$$

Substitute \(x = 2\) to find the y-coordinate:

$$y = (2)^2 - 4(2) + 7 = 4 - 8 + 7 = 3$$

The vertex is at \((2,\, 3)\).

Since \(a = 1 > 0\), the parabola opens upward, so the vertex is a minimum point.

Also, the discriminant is \(\Delta = 16 - 28 = -12 < 0\), confirming the parabola never crosses the x-axis. The minimum value of \(y\) is 3.

Problem 6 — Word Problem

A rectangle has an area of 48 square units, and its length is 2 units more than its width. Find the dimensions of the rectangle.

Solution

Let \(w\) represent the width. Then the length is \(w + 2\).

$$\text{Area} = w(w + 2) = 48$$ $$w^2 + 2w - 48 = 0$$

Factor: We need two numbers that multiply to \(-48\) and add to \(2\). Those are \(8\) and \(-6\).

$$(w + 8)(w - 6) = 0$$ $$w = -8 \quad \text{or} \quad w = 6$$

Since width must be positive, \(w = 6\).

Therefore: width = \(6\) units, length = \(6 + 2 = 8\) units.

Check: \(6 \times 8 = 48\;\checkmark\)
