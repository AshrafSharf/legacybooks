Chapter 12

Solving Everyday Problems

Translating real-world situations into algebraic equations and solving them

What You'll Learn

This final chapter brings together all the algebraic skills you have built throughout this book. You will learn to read word problems, translate them into equations, solve those equations, and verify your answers. The problems span everyday scenarios: numbers, ages, money, distance, mixtures, work, geometry, and investments.

  • Translate verbal expressions of mathematical relationships into algebraic symbols
  • Apply a four-step strategy: Read, Represent, Translate, Solve, Check
  • Solve number problems with one and two unknowns
  • Set up and solve age problems using time relationships
  • Formulate coin and money problems with multiple denominations
  • Tackle distance-rate-time problems: separation, closure, round trip, and overtake
  • Mix solutions, blends, or products using mixture equations
  • Model work-rate problems where multiple agents combine effort
  • Handle geometry problems by translating shapes into equations
  • Solve simple-interest investment problems with multiple accounts

The Problem-Solving Strategy

Every word problem, regardless of its specific topic, follows the same disciplined process. Master these steps and no problem will feel alien.

The Four Steps of Problem Solving
  1. Represent the unknowns. Assign a variable (usually \(x\)) to one unknown. Express all other unknowns in terms of that variable.
  2. Translate into an equation. Read the problem sentence by sentence and convert each relationship into algebraic form.
  3. Solve the equation. Use the algebraic techniques from previous chapters to find the value(s) of the unknowns.
  4. Check in the original problem. Substitute your answers back into the original word statement, not your equation (which might itself be wrong).
Common Mistake

Never check your answer in your own equation. If you set up the equation incorrectly, the solution will satisfy the equation but not the problem. Always go back to the original words.

Translating Words to Symbols

The first hurdle in word problems is converting English into algebra. The chart below maps common phrases to their mathematical operations.

SymbolKey WordsExample
\(+\) sum, plus, added to, increased by, more than "8 increased by 3" → \(8 + 3\)
\(-\) minus, less, decreased by, diminished by, exceeds "9 decreased by 4" → \(9 - 4\)
\(\times\) times, product, multiplied by, of "half of 8" → \(\tfrac{1}{2} \times 8\)
\(\div\) quotient, divided by, ratio "quotient of \(n\) and 3" → \(\tfrac{n}{3}\)
\(=\) equals, is, is equal to, was equal to, the result is "half of 8 is 4" → \(\tfrac{1}{2}(8) = 4\)
\(x\) a number, a certain number, unknown "a number added to 5" → \(x + 5\)
Key Insight

"Exceeds" and "Excess": The phrase "\(a\) exceeds \(b\) by \(c\)" means \(a - b = c\). Mentally replace exceeds with is greater than, and excess with difference. This small translation trick eliminates a very common source of errors.

Key Insight

Three ways to express the same relationship:

$$\text{larger} - \text{smaller} = \text{difference}$$ $$\text{larger} = \text{smaller} + \text{difference}$$ $$\text{smaller} = \text{larger} - \text{difference}$$

Choose whichever form makes your equation simplest to solve.

Number Problems

One Unknown

In a one-unknown number problem, one relationship defines the variable and a single equation captures the constraint.

Quick Example

Problem: Twice a certain number increased by 12 equals 26. Find the number.

Let \(n\) = the number.

$$2n + 12 = 26 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2n = 14 \;\Longrightarrow\; n = 7$$

Check: \(2(7) + 12 = 14 + 12 = 26\). Confirmed.

Two Unknowns

With two unknowns you need two relationships. You may either express both unknowns in terms of one letter (Method 1) or use two letters and solve a system (Method 2).

Quick Example

Problem: One number is 3 more than twice the other. Their sum is 18. Find them.

Let \(s\) = smaller number. Then the larger is \(2s + 3\).

$$s + (2s + 3) = 18 \;\Longrightarrow\; 3s + 3 = 18 \;\Longrightarrow\; s = 5$$

Larger: \(2(5) + 3 = 13\). Check: \(5 + 13 = 18\) and \(13 = 2(5) + 3\). Confirmed.

Age Problems

Age problems rely on one key fact: the difference between two people's ages stays constant forever. Two rules guide the setup:

Age Rules

Rule 1 (Future age): Add the given years to the present age.

$$\text{Age in } k \text{ years} = \text{present age} + k$$

Rule 2 (Past age): Subtract the given years from the present age.

$$\text{Age } k \text{ years ago} = \text{present age} - k$$
Example — Father and Son

Problem: A man is 9 times as old as his son. In 3 years the father will be only 5 times as old as his son. Find their ages.

Let \(x\) = son's age now. Then \(9x\) = father's age now.

In 3 years: son is \(x + 3\), father is \(9x + 3\).

$$9x + 3 = 5(x + 3)$$ $$9x + 3 = 5x + 15 \;\Longrightarrow\; 4x = 12 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = 3$$

Son is 3 years old, father is 27 years old.

Check: In 3 years they will be 6 and 30. Is \(30 = 5 \times 6\)? Yes.

Coin and Money Problems

The core principle: total value = (number of items) × (value per item). When you have two types of coins, you get two relationships: one for the count and one for the value.

Coin Problem Setup

If you have \(d\) dimes and \(q\) quarters:

$$\text{Count equation: } d + q = \text{total coins}$$ $$\text{Value equation: } 0.10d + 0.25q = \text{total value in dollars}$$

Or, to avoid decimals, work in cents:

$$10d + 25q = \text{total value in cents}$$

Distance-Rate-Time Problems

All motion problems rest on the fundamental relationship:

The Distance Formula
$$d = r \times t$$

where \(d\) = distance, \(r\) = rate (speed), and \(t\) = time. Rearranged:

$$r = \frac{d}{t} \qquad\qquad t = \frac{d}{r}$$

Units must be consistent. If rate is in mph, distance is in miles and time is in hours.

Motion problems come in four classic varieties:

1. Separation (Opposite Directions)

Two travelers leave the same point at the same time, heading in opposite directions. The sum of their distances equals the total separation.

2. Closure (Toward Each Other)

Two travelers start far apart and move toward each other. The sum of their distances equals the initial separation.

3. Round Trip

Same traveler, same route, different speeds each way. The key: distance going = distance returning.

4. Overtake (Same Direction)

A faster traveler starts later and catches up to a slower one. The key: both travel the same distance, but the first traveler has a head start in time.

Key Insight

Draw a diagram! For every motion problem, sketch the situation. Label starting points, directions, rates, and times. The diagram often makes the equation obvious.

Setting Up a Distance-Rate-Time Table

A table keeps the information organized:

RateTimeDistance = Rate × Time
Traveler 1\(r_1\)\(t_1\)\(r_1 \cdot t_1\)
Traveler 2\(r_2\)\(t_2\)\(r_2 \cdot t_2\)

Fill in what you know, use a variable for what you don't, then write an equation based on the problem type (sum of distances, equal distances, etc.).

Mixture Problems

Mixture problems appear in many guises — blending coffees, mixing chemical solutions, combining investments — but they all follow one principle:

The Mixture Principle
$$\text{Amount}_1 \times \text{Concentration}_1 + \text{Amount}_2 \times \text{Concentration}_2 = \text{Total Amount} \times \text{Final Concentration}$$

Work Problems

When multiple workers (or pipes, machines, etc.) combine their efforts, we use the concept of work rate: the fraction of a job completed per unit time.

Combined Work Rate Formula

If worker A can complete a job in \(a\) hours and worker B in \(b\) hours, their combined time \(t\) satisfies:

$$\frac{1}{a} + \frac{1}{b} = \frac{1}{t}$$

Each fraction represents a rate: the portion of the job done per hour.

More Problem Types

Percentage Problems

The relationship between percentage (\(P\)), base (\(B\)), and rate (\(R\)) is:

Percentage Formula
$$P = R \times B$$

Three variations: (1) Find the percentage: \(P = R \times B\). (2) Find the rate: \(R = P / B\). (3) Find the base: \(B = P / R\).

Geometry Problems

Translate perimeter, area, and angle relationships into equations. Common setups:

  • Perimeter of a rectangle: \(2l + 2w = P\), combined with a relationship between \(l\) and \(w\)
  • Triangle angles: \(A + B + C = 180°\), combined with given relationships
  • Complementary angles: \(x + y = 90°\)
  • Supplementary angles: \(x + y = 180°\)

Investment / Interest Problems

Simple Interest Formula
$$I = P \cdot r \cdot t$$

where \(I\) = interest earned, \(P\) = principal, \(r\) = annual rate (as decimal), \(t\) = time in years.

When money is split between two investments at different rates, you get a system:

$$P_1 + P_2 = \text{Total Principal}$$ $$r_1 P_1 + r_2 P_2 = \text{Total Interest}$$

Lever / Balance Problems

Lever Principle (Archimedes)
$$w_1 \times d_1 = w_2 \times d_2$$

where \(w_1, w_2\) are weights and \(d_1, d_2\) are distances from the fulcrum.

Worked Problems

Problem 1 — Consecutive Integers

Problem

The sum of three consecutive integers is 72. Find the three integers.

Solution

Step 1 — Represent: Let \(n\) = the first integer. Then the three consecutive integers are \(n\), \(n+1\), and \(n+2\).

Step 2 — Translate:

$$n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) = 72$$

Step 3 — Solve:

$$3n + 3 = 72$$ $$3n = 69$$ $$n = 23$$

The three integers are \(23\), \(24\), and \(25\).

Step 4 — Check: \(23 + 24 + 25 = 72\). Confirmed.

Problem 2 — Age Problem

Problem

Maria is 4 years older than twice Ana's age. The sum of their ages is 25. How old is each?

Solution

Step 1 — Represent: Let \(a\) = Ana's age. Then Maria's age is \(2a + 4\).

Step 2 — Translate:

$$a + (2a + 4) = 25$$

Step 3 — Solve:

$$3a + 4 = 25$$ $$3a = 21$$ $$a = 7$$

Ana is 7 years old. Maria is \(2(7) + 4 = \textbf{18 years old}\).

Step 4 — Check: Is Maria 4 more than twice Ana? \(2 \times 7 + 4 = 18\). Yes. Sum: \(7 + 18 = 25\). Confirmed.

Problem 3 — Coin Problem

Problem

A person has 15 coins consisting of dimes and quarters. The total value is $2.85. How many of each coin?

Solution

Step 1 — Represent: Let \(d\) = number of dimes, \(q\) = number of quarters.

Step 2 — Translate (working in cents):

$$d + q = 15 \quad \text{...(count)}$$ $$10d + 25q = 285 \quad \text{...(value)}$$

Step 3 — Solve: From the first equation, \(d = 15 - q\). Substitute:

$$10(15 - q) + 25q = 285$$ $$150 - 10q + 25q = 285$$ $$15q = 135$$ $$q = 9$$

So \(d = 15 - 9 = 6\).

There are 6 dimes and 9 quarters.

Step 4 — Check: Count: \(6 + 9 = 15\). Value: \(6 \times \$0.10 + 9 \times \$0.25 = \$0.60 + \$2.25 = \$2.85\). Confirmed.

Problem 4 — Distance (Separation)

Problem

Two cars leave the same point at the same time, traveling in opposite directions at 50 mph and 60 mph. How long until they are 330 miles apart?

Solution

Step 1 — Represent: Let \(t\) = time traveled (hours).

Step 2 — Translate: The sum of their distances equals 330 miles.

RateTimeDistance
Car A50 mph\(t\)\(50t\)
Car B60 mph\(t\)\(60t\)
$$50t + 60t = 330$$

Step 3 — Solve:

$$110t = 330$$ $$t = 3 \text{ hours}$$

Step 4 — Check: Car A travels \(50 \times 3 = 150\) miles. Car B travels \(60 \times 3 = 180\) miles. Total: \(150 + 180 = 330\) miles. Confirmed.

Problem 5 — Mixture Problem

Problem

How many liters of a 40% acid solution must be mixed with a 70% acid solution to produce 10 liters of a 50% solution?

Solution

Step 1 — Represent: Let \(x\) = liters of 40% solution. Then \((10 - x)\) = liters of 70% solution.

Step 2 — Translate: (acid from A) + (acid from B) = (acid in mixture)

$$0.40x + 0.70(10 - x) = 0.50(10)$$

Step 3 — Solve:

$$0.40x + 7.0 - 0.70x = 5.0$$ $$-0.30x = -2.0$$ $$x = \frac{2.0}{0.30} = \frac{20}{3} = 6\tfrac{2}{3} \text{ liters}$$

So use \(6\frac{2}{3}\) liters of 40% and \(3\frac{1}{3}\) liters of 70%.

Step 4 — Check: Acid from A: \(0.40 \times \frac{20}{3} = \frac{8}{3}\). Acid from B: \(0.70 \times \frac{10}{3} = \frac{7}{3}\). Total acid: \(\frac{8}{3} + \frac{7}{3} = 5\). Is \(5 = 0.50 \times 10\)? Yes. Confirmed.

Problem 6 — Work Problem

Problem

Pipe A can fill a pool in 6 hours. Pipe B can fill it in 4 hours. How long will it take both pipes working together?

Solution

Step 1 — Represent: Let \(t\) = time (hours) to fill the pool together.

Pipe A's rate: \(\frac{1}{6}\) of the pool per hour. Pipe B's rate: \(\frac{1}{4}\) of the pool per hour.

Step 2 — Translate:

$$\frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{t}$$

Step 3 — Solve: Find a common denominator (12):

$$\frac{2}{12} + \frac{3}{12} = \frac{1}{t}$$ $$\frac{5}{12} = \frac{1}{t}$$ $$t = \frac{12}{5} = 2.4 \text{ hours} = 2 \text{ hours } 24 \text{ minutes}$$

Step 4 — Check: In 2.4 hours, Pipe A fills \(\frac{2.4}{6} = 0.4\) of the pool. Pipe B fills \(\frac{2.4}{4} = 0.6\) of the pool. Together: \(0.4 + 0.6 = 1.0\) (the whole pool). Confirmed.

Problem 7 — Investment Problem

Problem

A total of $5,000 is invested in two accounts: one paying 5% annually and the other 8% annually. The total annual income from both investments is $340. How much is invested at each rate?

Solution

Step 1 — Represent: Let \(x\) = amount at 5%. Then \((5000 - x)\) = amount at 8%.

Step 2 — Translate:

$$0.05x + 0.08(5000 - x) = 340$$

Step 3 — Solve:

$$0.05x + 400 - 0.08x = 340$$ $$-0.03x = -60$$ $$x = 2000$$

Invested at 5%: $2,000. Invested at 8%: \(5000 - 2000 =\) $3,000.

Step 4 — Check: Interest from 5%: \(0.05 \times 2000 = \$100\). Interest from 8%: \(0.08 \times 3000 = \$240\). Total: \(\$100 + \$240 = \$340\). Confirmed.

Final Reminder

Always check your answer against the original word statement, not against the equation you wrote. A correct solution to a wrong equation is still a wrong answer!

Key Insights

Insight 1 — Meaning First

Before writing any symbols, make sure you understand what the words mean. "Decreased by," "diminished by," and "less" all signal subtraction. "Increased by," "added to," and "more than" all signal addition. Getting the operation wrong dooms the entire solution.

Insight 2 — Draw a Picture

For motion and mixture problems, a quick diagram transforms confusion into clarity. Label every quantity you know and every unknown. The equation often writes itself once the diagram is in front of you.

Insight 3 — Use a Table

Distance-rate-time problems, coin problems, and mixture problems all benefit from a table with columns for the known and unknown quantities. Fill in what you know, assign a variable to what you don't, and the relationships emerge naturally.

Insight 4 — One Variable When Possible

If two quantities are linked (e.g., their sum is 15), express both in terms of a single variable: \(d\) and \(15 - d\). This avoids the need for simultaneous equations and keeps the algebra simpler.

Insight 5 — The Check Is Non-Negotiable

Checking is not optional busywork — it is the only way to catch a modeling error. An algebraically correct solution to a wrongly formed equation gives a wrong answer. Always verify against the original words.
