Chapter 6

Exponents, Roots, and Radicals

Mastering the laws of exponents and the algebra of radicals

What You'll Learn

This chapter builds on your earlier work with powers and square roots, giving you a complete toolkit for manipulating exponents and radicals. By the end, you will be able to:

  • Apply the five laws of exponents to simplify algebraic expressions
  • Work with zero and negative exponents
  • Convert between standard notation and scientific notation
  • Connect radicals to fractional exponents
  • Simplify, add, subtract, multiply, and divide radicals
  • Rationalize denominators containing radicals
  • Solve simple radical equations

1. The Laws of Exponents

Recall that an exponent tells us how many times the base is used as a factor. For a real number \(a\) and a positive integer \(m\):

Definition — Positive Integer Exponent
$$a^m = \underbrace{a \cdot a \cdot a \cdots a}_{m \text{ factors}}$$

Five fundamental rules govern how exponents behave. Together they let us simplify nearly any expression that involves powers.

The Five Laws of Exponents
$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{1. Product Rule:}\quad & a^m \cdot a^n = a^{m+n} \\[6pt] \textbf{2. Power of a Power:}\quad & (a^m)^n = a^{mn} \\[6pt] \textbf{3. Quotient Rule:}\quad & \frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m-n} \quad (a \neq 0) \\[6pt] \textbf{4. Power of a Product:}\quad & (ab)^n = a^n \cdot b^n \\[6pt] \textbf{5. Power of a Quotient:}\quad & \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^n = \frac{a^n}{b^n} \quad (b \neq 0) \end{aligned}$$
Example

Simplify \(x^3 \cdot x^5\).

Solution: By the Product Rule, keep the base and add the exponents:

$$x^3 \cdot x^5 = x^{3+5} = x^8$$
Example

Simplify \((b^4)^3\).

Solution: By the Power of a Power rule, multiply the exponents:

$$(b^4)^3 = b^{4 \cdot 3} = b^{12}$$

2. Zero and Negative Exponents

The Zero Exponent

What happens when we divide equal powers? Using the Quotient Rule:

$$\frac{x^5}{x^5} = x^{5-5} = x^0$$

But any nonzero quantity divided by itself equals 1. So we define:

Definition — Zero Exponent
$$a^0 = 1 \qquad (a \neq 0)$$

Every nonzero base raised to the zero power equals 1. Thus \(7^0 = 1\), \(153^0 = 1\), and \((15x^4)^0 = 1\).

Common Mistake

Watch where the exponent applies! In \(15x^0\), the exponent zero applies only to \(x\), not to 15. So \(15x^0 = 15 \cdot 1 = 15\). Compare this with \((15x)^0 = 1\).

Negative Exponents

What if the exponent in the denominator is larger? For example:

$$\frac{x^2}{x^5} = \frac{x \cdot x}{x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x} = \frac{1}{x^3}$$

But by the Quotient Rule, \(\frac{x^2}{x^5} = x^{2-5} = x^{-3}\). This motivates the definition:

Definition — Negative Exponent
$$a^{-n} = \frac{1}{a^n} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \frac{1}{a^{-n}} = a^n \qquad (a \neq 0)$$

A negative exponent moves a factor between numerator and denominator, flipping the sign of the exponent.

Key Insight

Negative exponents do not make a number negative. They create a reciprocal. For instance, \(2^{-3} = \frac{1}{2^3} = \frac{1}{8}\), which is positive.

Example

Write \(v^{-4}\) using only positive exponents.

Solution:

$$v^{-4} = \frac{1}{v^4}$$

3. Scientific Notation

Very large or very small numbers are unwieldy to write in standard form. Scientific notation uses powers of 10 to express them compactly.

Definition — Scientific Notation

A number is in scientific notation when it is written as:

$$a \times 10^n \qquad \text{where } 1 \le |a| < 10 \text{ and } n \text{ is an integer}$$

Converting to Scientific Notation

  • Large numbers — move the decimal point left; the exponent is positive.
  • Small numbers — move the decimal point right; the exponent is negative.
Examples
$$3{,}400{,}000 = 3.4 \times 10^6 \qquad \text{(decimal moved 6 places left)}$$ $$0.0025 = 2.5 \times 10^{-3} \qquad \text{(decimal moved 3 places right)}$$
Key Insight

The exponent on 10 counts the number of places the decimal point moved. A positive exponent means the original number is large (greater than 10); a negative exponent means it is small (less than 1).

4. Roots and Radicals

Raising a number to a power and extracting a root are inverse operations. The notation \(\sqrt[m]{a}\) means "the \(m\)th root of \(a\)."

Definition — The nth Root

If \(n^m = a\), then \(n = \sqrt[m]{a}\).

The symbol \(\sqrt{\phantom{x}}\) is the radical sign, \(a\) is the radicand, and \(m\) is the index. When no index is written, it is assumed to be 2 (square root).

Some examples to build intuition:

Examples
$$\sqrt{9} = 3 \quad \text{because } 3^2 = 9$$ $$\sqrt{25} = 5 \quad \text{because } 5^2 = 25$$ $$\sqrt[3]{8} = 2 \quad \text{because } 2^3 = 8$$ $$\sqrt[3]{-8} = -2 \quad \text{because } (-2)^3 = -8$$ $$\sqrt[4]{81} = 3 \quad \text{because } 3^4 = 81$$
Definition — Principal Square Root

\(\sqrt{n}\) denotes the positive (principal) square root of \(n\). The negative square root is written \(-\sqrt{n}\). For example, \(\sqrt{16} = 4\), while \(-\sqrt{16} = -4\).

Roots of Fractions

The root of a fraction is found by taking the root of the numerator and the root of the denominator separately:

Root of a Fraction
$$\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}} \qquad (b > 0)$$
Example
$$\sqrt{\frac{9}{16}} = \frac{\sqrt{9}}{\sqrt{16}} = \frac{3}{4}$$

5. Fractional Exponents

Fractional exponents provide an alternative notation for radicals. The key observation is:

$$x^{1/2} \cdot x^{1/2} = x^{1/2 + 1/2} = x^1 = x$$

Since \(\sqrt{x} \cdot \sqrt{x} = x\) as well, it follows that \(x^{1/2} = \sqrt{x}\). More generally:

Definition — Fractional Exponents
$$x^{1/n} = \sqrt[n]{x}$$

And for a fractional exponent with a numerator other than 1:

$$x^{m/n} = \sqrt[n]{x^m} = \left(\sqrt[n]{x}\right)^m$$

The denominator of the fraction is the index of the radical; the numerator is the power of the radicand.

Key Insight — Two Paths, Same Answer

When computing \(a^{m/n}\), you can either take the \(n\)th root first and then raise to the \(m\)th power, or raise to the \(m\)th power first and then take the \(n\)th root. Choosing the root first usually keeps the numbers smaller.

Examples
$$4^{1/2} = \sqrt{4} = 2$$ $$27^{1/3} = \sqrt[3]{27} = 3$$ $$8^{2/3} = \left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^2 = 2^2 = 4$$ $$(16a^8)^{1/2} = \sqrt{16a^8} = 4a^4 \quad (a > 0)$$

Absolute Value and Even Roots

When taking an even root of an even power, the result is always non-negative. To guarantee this, we use absolute value:

Even Root of an Even Power
$$\sqrt{x^2} = |x| \qquad \text{or equivalently} \qquad (x^2)^{1/2} = |x|$$

For example, \(\sqrt{(-3)^2} = \sqrt{9} = 3 = |-3|\).

6. Simplifying Radicals

A radical can be simplified if any of the following apply:

  • The radicand is a perfect square (e.g., \(\sqrt{36} = 6\)).
  • The radicand contains a factor that is a perfect square (e.g., \(\sqrt{48}\)).
  • The radicand is a fraction (e.g., \(\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}\)).
Product and Quotient Rules for Radicals
$$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} \qquad \text{and} \qquad \sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}$$

Procedure: Simplifying a Square Root

  1. Factor the radicand to find the largest perfect-square factor.
  2. Apply the product rule: \(\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{b}\).
  3. Take the square root of the perfect square.
Example

Simplify \(\sqrt{48}\).

$$\sqrt{48} = \sqrt{16 \cdot 3} = \sqrt{16} \cdot \sqrt{3} = 4\sqrt{3}$$
Example

Simplify \(\sqrt{50}\).

$$\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{25} \cdot \sqrt{2} = 5\sqrt{2}$$

7. Adding and Subtracting Radicals

Radicals can be combined by addition or subtraction only when they are like radicals — that is, when they have the same index and the same radicand. The process uses the distributive law:

Combining Like Radicals
$$a\sqrt{x} + b\sqrt{x} = (a + b)\sqrt{x}$$
Example

Simplify \(4\sqrt{5} + 3\sqrt{5} - 2\sqrt{5}\).

$$4\sqrt{5} + 3\sqrt{5} - 2\sqrt{5} = (4 + 3 - 2)\sqrt{5} = 5\sqrt{5}$$
Key Insight

Sometimes radicals that look different become like radicals after simplification. Always simplify each radical first, then combine.

Example

Simplify \(3\sqrt{8} - \sqrt{50} + 6\sqrt{32}\).

First, simplify each term:

$$3\sqrt{8} = 3\sqrt{4 \cdot 2} = 3 \cdot 2\sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt{2}$$ $$\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \cdot 2} = 5\sqrt{2}$$ $$6\sqrt{32} = 6\sqrt{16 \cdot 2} = 6 \cdot 4\sqrt{2} = 24\sqrt{2}$$

Now combine:

$$6\sqrt{2} - 5\sqrt{2} + 24\sqrt{2} = 25\sqrt{2}$$

8. Multiplying and Dividing Radicals

Multiplying Radicals

Product Rule for Radicals
$$\sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$$

Also: \((\sqrt{a})^2 = a\).

Examples
$$\sqrt{3} \cdot \sqrt{4} = \sqrt{12} = 2\sqrt{3}$$ $$\sqrt{2} \cdot \sqrt{18} = \sqrt{36} = 6$$ $$5\sqrt{2} \cdot 3\sqrt{2} = 15\sqrt{4} = 15 \cdot 2 = 30$$

Multiplying Polynomials with Radicals

Use the distributive law (or FOIL for two binomials), then simplify.

Example — Monomial times Binomial
$$\sqrt{3}\left(7 - \sqrt{3}\right) = 7\sqrt{3} - \sqrt{3}\cdot\sqrt{3} = 7\sqrt{3} - 3$$
Example — Binomial times Binomial
$$(4 + \sqrt{2})(2 - \sqrt{2}) = 8 - 4\sqrt{2} + 2\sqrt{2} - \sqrt{4} = 8 - 2\sqrt{2} - 2 = 6 - 2\sqrt{2}$$

Dividing Radicals

To divide radicals, divide the coefficients and divide the radicands, then simplify:

Example
$$\frac{6\sqrt{24}}{2\sqrt{3}} = \frac{6}{2} \cdot \sqrt{\frac{24}{3}} = 3\sqrt{8} = 3 \cdot 2\sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt{2}$$

9. Rationalizing Denominators

A fraction is not in simplest form if its denominator contains a radical. Rationalizing eliminates radicals from the denominator.

Simple Denominators

Multiply numerator and denominator by the radical in the denominator:

Example
$$\frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3}$$

Binomial Denominators — Using the Conjugate

When the denominator is a binomial involving a square root, multiply by its conjugate — the same expression with the opposite middle sign. This uses the difference-of-squares identity:

$$(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2$$
Rationalizing a Binomial Denominator
$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}} = \frac{\sqrt{a} - \sqrt{b}}{a - b}$$
Example

Rationalize \(\displaystyle\frac{3 + \sqrt{2}}{2 - \sqrt{2}}\).

Solution: Multiply by the conjugate \(\frac{2 + \sqrt{2}}{2 + \sqrt{2}}\):

$$\frac{(3 + \sqrt{2})(2 + \sqrt{2})}{(2 - \sqrt{2})(2 + \sqrt{2})} = \frac{6 + 3\sqrt{2} + 2\sqrt{2} + 2}{4 - 2} = \frac{8 + 5\sqrt{2}}{2}$$

10. Solving Radical Equations

A radical equation is an equation in which the variable appears under a radical sign. The key technique is to isolate the radical, then square both sides.

Procedure for Solving Radical Equations
  1. Isolate the radical term on one side of the equation.
  2. Square both sides to eliminate the radical.
  3. Solve the resulting equation.
  4. Check your answer in the original equation (squaring can introduce extraneous solutions).
Example

Solve \(\sqrt{2x} - 2 = 4\).

Step 1: Isolate the radical: \(\sqrt{2x} = 6\).

Step 2: Square both sides: \(2x = 36\).

Step 3: Solve: \(x = 18\).

Step 4: Check: \(\sqrt{2 \cdot 18} - 2 = \sqrt{36} - 2 = 6 - 2 = 4\). ✓

Caution — Extraneous Roots

Since \(\sqrt{x}\) always means the positive square root, equations like \(\sqrt{x} = -3\) have no solution. Always check your answer. If it fails the check, it is an extraneous root.

Worked Problems

Problem 1

Simplify \((2x^3 y^2)^4\).

Solution

Apply the Power of a Product rule: distribute the exponent 4 to each factor.

$$(2x^3 y^2)^4 = 2^4 \cdot (x^3)^4 \cdot (y^2)^4$$

Evaluate each term using the Power of a Power rule:

$$= 16 \cdot x^{12} \cdot y^{8} = 16x^{12}y^{8}$$
Problem 2

Rewrite \(3x^{-2}\) using only positive exponents.

Solution

The negative exponent applies only to \(x\), not to the coefficient 3. Move \(x^{-2}\) to the denominator:

$$3x^{-2} = 3 \cdot \frac{1}{x^2} = \frac{3}{x^2}$$
Problem 3

Write \(0.00047\) in scientific notation.

Solution

Move the decimal point to the right until we have a number between 1 and 10:

$$0.00047 = 4.7 \times 10^{-4}$$

We moved the decimal point 4 places to the right, so the exponent is \(-4\).

Problem 4

Simplify \(\sqrt{72}\).

Solution

Find the largest perfect-square factor of 72. Since \(72 = 36 \times 2\):

$$\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{36} \cdot \sqrt{2} = 6\sqrt{2}$$
Problem 5

Rationalize the denominator: \(\displaystyle\frac{5}{\sqrt{3}}\).

Solution

Multiply numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt{3}\):

$$\frac{5}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{5}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{5\sqrt{3}}{3}$$
Problem 6

Evaluate \(8^{2/3}\).

Solution

The denominator 3 tells us to take the cube root; the numerator 2 tells us to square the result:

$$8^{2/3} = \left(\sqrt[3]{8}\right)^2 = 2^2 = 4$$

Alternatively: \(8^{2/3} = \sqrt[3]{8^2} = \sqrt[3]{64} = 4\). Same answer either way.
