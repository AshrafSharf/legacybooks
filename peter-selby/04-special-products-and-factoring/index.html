Chapter 4

Special Products and Factoring

Reverse the multiplication process — break expressions into their building blocks

What You'll Learn

Factoring is the reverse of multiplication: instead of combining factors into a product, you decompose a product back into its factors. Mastering these patterns is essential for simplifying expressions, solving equations, and working with fractions in algebra.

  • Find common monomial factors of polynomial expressions
  • Recognize and factor the difference of two squares
  • Identify and factor perfect square trinomials
  • Factor general trinomials of the form \(x^2 + bx + c\)
  • Factor trinomials with leading coefficient \(a \neq 1\)
  • Understand the sum and difference of cubes
  • Apply a complete factoring strategy to any polynomial
  • Square binomials and find products of binomials by inspection

What Factoring Involves

Factoring is expressing a number or algebraic expression as a product of its component factors. The process is the reverse of multiplication.

🔑 Definition — Factoring

Given a product, factoring means finding two or more expressions that multiply together to give that product.

$$\text{Multiplication: } \underbrace{3 \times 7}_{\text{factors}} = \underbrace{21}_{\text{product}} \qquad \text{Factoring: } \underbrace{21}_{\text{product}} = \underbrace{3 \times 7}_{\text{factors}}$$

The relationship between multiplication, division, and factoring:

📋 Three Related Operations
$$\begin{aligned} \textbf{Multiplication:} &\quad \text{factor} \times \text{factor} = \text{product} \\[6pt] \textbf{Division:} &\quad \text{product} \div \text{factor} = \text{factor} \\[6pt] \textbf{Factoring:} &\quad \text{product} = \text{factor} \times \text{factor} \end{aligned}$$

For example, since \(a(a + 4) = a^2 + 4a\), we can say that \(a\) and \((a + 4)\) are factors of \(a^2 + 4a\). In factoring, both the divisor and the quotient must be found — it is a special kind of division.

Common Monomial Factor

The first and most important step in any factoring problem is to look for factors common to every term of the expression. Always extract the greatest common factor (GCF) first.

📋 Common Factor Rule
$$ab + ac = a(b + c)$$

Pull out the largest factor shared by every term. This reverses the distributive law.

🔑 Finding the Highest Common Factor

To factor a polynomial, identify the highest common numerical factor and the smallest exponent of each common literal factor across all terms. Together, these form the greatest common monomial factor.

✅ Example

Factor \(6x^5 + 12x^4 - 18x^3\).

All numerical coefficients are divisible by 6. The smallest power of \(x\) is \(x^3\). So the GCF is \(6x^3\).

$$6x^5 + 12x^4 - 18x^3 = 6x^3(x^2 + 2x - 3)$$
💡 Key Insight

Always factor out the GCF first, before attempting any other factoring technique. A polynomial that looks complicated may become a recognizable pattern once the common factor is removed.

Prime Expressions

A prime number is a whole number greater than 1 whose only whole number factors are 1 and itself (e.g., 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 31). Similarly, a polynomial is prime (or irreducible) if it cannot be factored further over the integers. Monomials are already considered prime expressions.

When factoring, continue until all factors are prime expressions — this is called complete factoring.

Squaring Monomials and Finding Square Roots

Squaring a Monomial

📋 Rule — Squaring a Monomial

Square the numerical coefficient, keep each literal factor, and double every exponent.

$$(-3x^2y^3)^2 = 9x^4y^6$$

Remember: even powers of negative numbers are always positive, so \((-3)^2 = 9\).

Finding the Principal Square Root

📋 Rule — Square Root of a Monomial

Take the principal (positive) square root of the numerical coefficient, keep each literal factor, and halve every exponent.

$$\sqrt{36a^4b^6} = 6a^2b^3$$
🔑 Terminology
  • The symbol \(\sqrt{\phantom{x}}\) is the radical sign
  • The number under the radical is the radicand
  • The combination of radical sign and radicand is a radical
  • The positive square root is the principal square root
  • Every positive number has two square roots: \(+\) and \(-\)

Multiplying Binomials by Inspection

Binomials can be multiplied mentally using the FOIL method (First, Outer, Inner, Last), which is a horizontal shortcut for the vertical multiplication you already know.

📋 FOIL Method
$$(ax + b)(cx + d) = \underbrace{ac}_{\text{First}} x^2 + \underbrace{(ad + bc)}_{\text{Outer + Inner}} x + \underbrace{bd}_{\text{Last}}$$
✅ Example

Multiply \((2x + 3)(x + 2)\):

$$\begin{aligned} \text{First: } &\quad 2x \cdot x = 2x^2 \\ \text{Cross products: } &\quad 3 \cdot x + 2x \cdot 2 = 3x + 4x = 7x \\ \text{Last: } &\quad 3 \cdot 2 = 6 \\[6pt] (2x + 3)(x + 2) &= 2x^2 + 7x + 6 \end{aligned}$$

The sum of the outer and inner products (the cross products) gives the middle term. With practice, you will write the answer directly — this is called finding the product by inspection.

Square of a Binomial

Squaring a binomial is one of the most common operations in algebra, and its result follows a distinctive pattern.

📋 Perfect Square Formulas
$$(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$$ $$(a - b)^2 = a^2 - 2ab + b^2$$

The procedure: (1) square the first term, (2) double the product of both terms (keeping the sign), and (3) square the second term.

⚠️ Common Mistake

Do NOT write \((a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2\). The middle term \(2ab\) is essential and must not be forgotten!

$$(a + b)^2 \neq a^2 + b^2$$
✅ Example

Square \((2x - 3)\):

$$\begin{aligned} (2x - 3)^2 &= (2x)^2 + 2(2x)(-3) + (-3)^2 \\ &= 4x^2 - 12x + 9 \end{aligned}$$

Geometric Proof: Area Model

The formula \((a+b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2\) has a beautiful geometric interpretation. A square with side length \((a+b)\) can be divided into four regions:

The total area of the large square is \((a+b)^2\). It equals the sum of the four regions: one \(a^2\) square, two \(ab\) rectangles, and one \(b^2\) square.

The Perfect Square Trinomial

Reversing the square-of-a-binomial formulas gives us a powerful factoring pattern. A trinomial is a perfect square if it matches either of these forms:

📋 Perfect Square Trinomial Patterns
$$a^2 + 2ab + b^2 = (a + b)^2$$ $$a^2 - 2ab + b^2 = (a - b)^2$$
🔑 How to Recognize a Perfect Square Trinomial

A trinomial (arranged in descending or ascending power) is a perfect square if:

  1. The first and third terms are positive perfect squares
  2. The middle term equals twice the product of the square roots of the first and third terms
✅ Example

Factor \(x^2 + 10x + 25\).

\(x^2\) is the square of \(x\). \(25\) is the square of \(5\). Is \(10x = 2 \cdot x \cdot 5\)? Yes!

$$x^2 + 10x + 25 = (x + 5)^2$$

The positive middle term tells us the binomial has a plus sign.

✅ Example

Factor \(9x^2 - 6x + 1\).

\(9x^2 = (3x)^2\) and \(1 = 1^2\). Check: \(2 \cdot 3x \cdot 1 = 6x\). The middle term is \(-6x\), so:

$$9x^2 - 6x + 1 = (3x - 1)^2$$
⚠️ Watch Out

\(x^2 + x + 1\) is not a perfect square because the middle term would need to be \(2x\), not \(x\). Similarly, \(z^2 + 12z - 36\) fails because the third term is negative — both the first and third terms must be positive.

Product of Sum and Difference

When you multiply the sum and difference of the same two terms, the cross products cancel and the middle term vanishes:

📋 Sum and Difference Pattern
$$(a + b)(a - b) = a^2 - b^2$$

The product equals the square of the first term minus the square of the second term.

✅ Example
$$(x + 3)(x - 3) = x^2 - 9$$ $$(2ax + 3)(2ax - 3) = 4a^2x^2 - 9$$
💡 Arithmetic Shortcut

This pattern can speed up mental arithmetic! For example:

$$35 \times 25 = (30 + 5)(30 - 5) = 30^2 - 5^2 = 900 - 25 = 875$$

Factoring the Difference of Two Squares

Reversing the sum-and-difference product gives us a factoring rule for any expression that is the difference of two perfect squares.

📋 Difference of Two Squares
$$a^2 - b^2 = (a + b)(a - b)$$

Step 1: Take the square root of each square.
Step 2: Write the sum as one factor and the difference as the other.

✅ Example

Factor \(c^2 - 9y^2\):

$$\sqrt{c^2} = c, \quad \sqrt{9y^2} = 3y$$ $$c^2 - 9y^2 = (c + 3y)(c - 3y)$$
⚠️ Cannot Factor the Sum of Two Squares

\(a^2 + b^2\) cannot be factored over the real numbers. The difference-of-squares pattern requires a minus sign between the two squares.

$$k^2 + 25 \quad \text{is prime (cannot be factored)}$$
💡 Look for Nested Differences

Sometimes one of the factors is itself a difference of two squares and can be factored again:

$$1 - 81r^4 = (1 + 9r^2)(1 - 9r^2) = (1 + 9r^2)(1 + 3r)(1 - 3r)$$

Factoring Trinomials

Since the product of two binomials is usually a trinomial, factoring a trinomial means finding the two binomials that were multiplied. This involves observation, logic, and sometimes trial and error.

Case 1: Leading Coefficient = 1

📋 Trinomial with \(x^2\) Leading Term
$$x^2 + bx + c = (x + m)(x + n)$$

where \(m \cdot n = c\) and \(m + n = b\). Find two numbers whose product is the constant term and whose sum is the coefficient of the middle term.

✅ Example

Factor \(a^2 + 5a + 6\):

We need two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 5. Those are 2 and 3.

$$a^2 + 5a + 6 = (a + 2)(a + 3)$$
✅ Example

Factor \(x^2 - 2x - 24\):

We need two numbers that multiply to \(-24\) and add to \(-2\). Try \(+4\) and \(-6\): their product is \(-24\) and their sum is \(-2\). ✓

$$x^2 - 2x - 24 = (x + 4)(x - 6)$$
💡 Sign Clues for the Binomial Factors
  • Third term positive, middle positive: both binomial signs are \(+\)
  • Third term positive, middle negative: both binomial signs are \(-\)
  • Third term negative: signs are opposite (one \(+\), one \(-\))

Case 2: Leading Coefficient \(\neq\) 1

📋 General Trinomial \(ax^2 + bx + c\)

When \(a \neq 1\), use trial and error: combine the possible factors of \(a\) and \(c\) to produce the correct middle term. The sign of the third term tells you whether the binomial signs are alike or opposite.

✅ Example

Factor \(4x^2 - 8x - 21\):

Factors of 4: \(1 \times 4\) or \(2 \times 2\). Factors of 21: \(1 \times 21\), \(3 \times 7\). The minus last term means opposite signs. Try \((2x + 3)(2x - 7)\):

$$2x \cdot (-7) + 3 \cdot 2x = -14x + 6x = -8x \quad \checkmark$$ $$4x^2 - 8x - 21 = (2x + 3)(2x - 7)$$

Sum and Difference of Cubes

Beyond squares, algebra also provides patterns for factoring expressions involving cubes. These are worth memorizing.

📋 Sum of Cubes
$$a^3 + b^3 = (a + b)(a^2 - ab + b^2)$$
📋 Difference of Cubes
$$a^3 - b^3 = (a - b)(a^2 + ab + b^2)$$
💡 Memory Aid: SOAP

For \(a^3 \pm b^3 = (a \quad \square \quad b)(a^2 \quad \square \quad ab + b^2)\), the signs follow the pattern Same, Opposite, Always Positive:

  • First factor: Same sign as the original
  • Second factor, first operation: Opposite sign
  • Second factor, last term: Always Positive
✅ Example

Factor \(8x^3 - 27\):

Recognize \(8x^3 = (2x)^3\) and \(27 = 3^3\). Apply the difference-of-cubes formula:

$$8x^3 - 27 = (2x - 3)\bigl((2x)^2 + (2x)(3) + 3^2\bigr) = (2x - 3)(4x^2 + 6x + 9)$$

Complete Factoring Strategy

When faced with any polynomial to factor, follow this systematic approach:

📋 Factoring Flowchart
  1. Extract the GCF — Always look for a common monomial factor first and pull it out.
  2. Count the terms:
    • 2 terms (binomial): Check for difference of squares \((a^2 - b^2)\), sum of cubes \((a^3 + b^3)\), or difference of cubes \((a^3 - b^3)\).
    • 3 terms (trinomial): Check for perfect square trinomial, then try standard trinomial factoring.
    • 4+ terms: Try factoring by grouping.
  3. Factor completely — Check each factor to see if it can be factored further. Continue until all factors are prime.
💡 Key Insight

A polynomial can be factored if: (1) it contains a common monomial factor, (2) it is a binomial that is the difference of two squares, or (3) it is a trinomial that is either a perfect square or can be factored by trial and error. Always remove the GCF first — it simplifies everything that follows.

Worked Problems

📝 Problem 1

Factor: \(\;6x^2y + 9xy^2\)

✅ Solution

Step 1: Identify the GCF of the two terms.

  • Numerical: GCF of 6 and 9 is 3
  • Literal: Both terms contain \(x\) (smallest power \(x^1\)) and \(y\) (smallest power \(y^1\))
  • GCF = \(3xy\)

Step 2: Divide each term by the GCF:

$$\frac{6x^2y}{3xy} = 2x, \qquad \frac{9xy^2}{3xy} = 3y$$

Result:

$$6x^2y + 9xy^2 = 3xy(2x + 3y)$$
📝 Problem 2

Factor: \(\;x^2 - 16\)

✅ Solution

Recognize this as a difference of two squares:

$$x^2 - 16 = x^2 - 4^2$$

Apply the formula \(a^2 - b^2 = (a+b)(a-b)\):

$$x^2 - 16 = (x + 4)(x - 4)$$
📝 Problem 3

Factor: \(\;x^2 + 6x + 9\)

✅ Solution

Check for a perfect square trinomial:

  • \(x^2 = (x)^2\) ✓
  • \(9 = (3)^2\) ✓
  • Middle term: \(2 \cdot x \cdot 3 = 6x\) ✓

All three conditions are met, with a positive middle term:

$$x^2 + 6x + 9 = (x + 3)^2$$
📝 Problem 4

Factor: \(\;x^2 + 5x + 6\)

✅ Solution

Find two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to 5:

  • \(1 \times 6 = 6\), \(1 + 6 = 7\) ✗
  • \(2 \times 3 = 6\), \(2 + 3 = 5\) ✓
$$x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3)$$
📝 Problem 5

Factor: \(\;2x^2 + 7x + 3\)

✅ Solution

The leading coefficient is 2, so we use trial and error.

Factors of 2: \(2 \times 1\). Factors of 3: \(3 \times 1\). Both signs are positive (all terms positive).

Try \((2x + 1)(x + 3)\):

$$\text{Cross products: } 2x \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot x = 6x + x = 7x \quad \checkmark$$ $$2x^2 + 7x + 3 = (2x + 1)(x + 3)$$
📝 Problem 6

Factor completely: \(\;3x^3 - 12x\)

✅ Solution

Step 1: Extract the GCF. Both terms share \(3x\):

$$3x^3 - 12x = 3x(x^2 - 4)$$

Step 2: Recognize \(x^2 - 4\) as a difference of two squares:

$$x^2 - 4 = (x + 2)(x - 2)$$

Step 3: Combine for the complete factorization:

$$3x^3 - 12x = 3x(x + 2)(x - 2)$$

All factors are now prime — we are done.

💡 The Golden Rule of Factoring

Always extract the GCF first. Then check for special patterns (difference of squares, perfect square trinomial). Then try trinomial factoring by trial and error. Finally, verify that every factor is prime.
