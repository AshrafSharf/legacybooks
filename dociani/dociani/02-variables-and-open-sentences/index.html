2-1 · Evaluating Algebraic Expressions

Just as the word "vegetables" stands for any element of a set of edible plants, a variable is a letter that stands for any element of a specified set of numbers. For instance, we can describe the set of multiples of 3:

$$A = \{0,\; 3,\; 6,\; 9,\; \dots\} = \{3n \mid n \in \{0,1,2,3,\dots\}\}$$

Here \(n\) is a variable. Each specific value you substitute for \(n\) gives one element of set \(A\).

Definition — Variable

A variable is a symbol that may represent any of the elements of a specified set. The set whose elements serve as replacements is called the replacement set (or domain) of the variable. A variable with exactly one value is called a constant.

Multiplication Notation

When a product involves a variable, the multiplication symbol is typically omitted:

  • \(3 \times n\) is written \(3n\) (read "three times \(n\)")
  • \(a \times b\) is written \(ab\) (read "\(a\) times \(b\)")
  • A raised dot may also indicate multiplication: \(2 \cdot n\) means \(2 \times n\)
Caution

With two specific numerals, you cannot omit the symbol: \(8 \times 7\) must be written \(8 \cdot 7\) or \(8(7)\), not \(87\), which means eighty-seven.

Definition — Key Terms

A variable expression (or open expression) is an expression containing a variable—its value is "left open" until you specify the variable's value.

A term is an algebraic expression written as a product or quotient of numerals or variables or both. For example, \(7\), \(2d\), \(3xy\), and \(\dfrac{m}{n-1}\) are all terms.

Evaluating with a Replacement Set

To evaluate an expression means to determine the number it represents for each value of the variable. If \(r \in \{2, 3, 5, 8\}\), we evaluate \(\frac{1}{2}r\) and \(5r\):

\(r\)\(\tfrac{1}{2}r\)\(5r\)
2\(\tfrac{1}{2}(2) = 1\)\(5(2) = 10\)
3\(\tfrac{1}{2}(3) = \tfrac{3}{2}\)\(5(3) = 15\)
5\(\tfrac{1}{2}(5) = \tfrac{5}{2}\)\(5(5) = 25\)
8\(\tfrac{1}{2}(8) = 4\)\(5(8) = 40\)

2-2 · Factors, Coefficients, and Exponents

When two or more numbers are multiplied, each is called a factor of the product. For example, 3 and 7 are factors of 21; the product \(6x\) has factors 1, 2, 3, 6, \(x\), \(2x\), \(3x\), and \(6x\) itself.

Definition — Coefficient

Each factor of a product is the coefficient of the product of the other factors. In the product \(\frac{1}{2}xy\), the number \(\frac{1}{2}\) is the coefficient of \(xy\), the expression \(\frac{1}{2}x\) is the coefficient of \(y\), and \(\frac{1}{2}y\) is the coefficient of \(x\). The numerical coefficient is the numerical part of a term. The coefficient of \(a\) is 1, since \(a = 1 \cdot a\).

Exponents and Powers

When a number appears more than once as a factor, we use an exponent to show how many times the base is used:

$$s^2 = s \cdot s \qquad\quad x^3 = x \cdot x \cdot x \qquad\quad x^4 = x \cdot x \cdot x \cdot x$$
Key Insight — Exponent vs. Coefficient

Exponent tells how many times the base is used as a factor:

$$s^2 = s \cdot s$$

When \(s = 15\): \(s^2 = 15 \cdot 15 = 225\)

Coefficient is itself a factor:

$$2s = 2 \cdot s$$

When \(s = 15\): \(2s = 2 \cdot 15 = 30\)

Grouping Symbols Matter

The placement of parentheses determines the base of the exponent:

  • \(3a^2 = 3 \cdot a \cdot a\) — only \(a\) is squared
  • \((3a)^2 = 3a \cdot 3a = 9a^2\) — the entire product \(3a\) is squared

Compare with numbers: \(4 \cdot 5^3 = 4 \cdot 125 = 500\), but \((4 \cdot 5)^3 = 20^3 = 8000\).

2-3 · Solving Open Sentences

Consider the sentence: "\(w\) is a city in Texas." As written, it is neither true nor false. Replace \(w\) with "Dallas" and it becomes true; replace with "New York" and it becomes false. A sentence containing a variable is called an open sentence.

Definition — Open Sentence

An algebraic sentence is a statement composed of algebraic expressions related by one of the symbols \(=\), \(\neq\), \(>\), \(<\), \(\le\), or \(\ge\).

  • A sentence using \(=\) is called an equation.
  • A sentence using \(\neq\), \(>\), \(<\), \(\le\), or \(\ge\) is called an inequality.

Equations: Left Member and Right Member

Solution Set and Roots

Definition — Solution Set

The solution set of an open sentence is the subset of the domain consisting of all values that make the sentence true. Each member of the solution set is called a root. Determining the solution set is called solving the open sentence.

Example: Solve \(3x + 1 = 16\), where \(x \in \{5, 6\}\).

Try x = 5

\(3(5) + 1 = 16\)
\(15 + 1 = 16\)
\(16 = 16\) ✓ True

Try x = 6

\(3(6) + 1 = 16\)
\(18 + 1 = 16\)
\(19 = 16\) ✗ False

Therefore the solution set is \(\{5\}\), and 5 is a root of the equation.

Inequalities

Now consider the inequality \(3x + 1 > 16\) with \(x \in \{5, 6\}\):

  • \(x = 5\): \(16 > 16\) is False
  • \(x = 6\): \(19 > 16\) is True

The solution set is \(\{6\}\).

2-4 · From Symbols to Words

A central skill in algebra is interpreting algebraic expressions in English. The expression \(x + 1\) might mean:

  • "A number increased by 1" or "one more than \(x\)"
  • "Ron is a year older than his sister Sue. If \(x\) is Sue's age, then \(x + 1\) is Ron's age."

The expression \(2w - 3\) means "the difference between twice a number \(w\) and 3." In context: "The length of a room is 3 feet less than twice its width."

Key Insight — Multiple Interpretations

Every algebraic expression has many possible English interpretations. Part of mathematical thinking is choosing the interpretation that fits the context of a problem. Note how the domain may change with context: if \(x\) represents Sue's age, then \(x \neq 0\) (since age is positive), but if \(x\) is simply "a number," the domain may include 0.

2-5 · From Words to Symbols

In solving problems, your first task is to translate English phrases into algebraic language. Here is a reference for common translations:

Translation Guide
English PhraseAlgebraic Expression
a number increased by 5\(n + 5\)
5 less than a number\(n - 5\)
twice a number\(2n\)
one-third of a number\(\dfrac{n}{3}\)
4 fewer than one-third of \(s\)\(\dfrac{s}{3} - 4\)
3 more than twice a number\(2n + 3\)
the square of a number\(n^2\)
Key Insight — "Less Than" Reverses Order

"5 less than \(n\)" is \(n - 5\), not \(5 - n\). The phrase "less than" tells you to subtract from the number that follows it. Similarly, "3 fewer than twice \(w\)" is \(2w - 3\).

2-6 · Solving Problems with Open Sentences

In a "word problem," you are told how certain numbers are related. To find the unknown numbers, you translate the problem into algebra and solve. Dolciani outlines a four-step method:

The Four-Step Method
Step 1. Choose a variable with an appropriate replacement set, and use it to represent each described number. Step 2. Form an open sentence by using the facts given in the problem. Step 3. Find the solution set of the open sentence. Step 4. Check your answer with the words of the problem.

Worked Problems

Problem 1 — Evaluating Expressions (Section 2-1)

Evaluate \(2x^2 + 4x + 5\) when \(x = 3\).

Solution

Substitute \(x = 3\) into each term:

$$2x^2 + 4x + 5 = 2(3)^2 + 4(3) + 5$$ $$= 2(9) + 12 + 5$$ $$= 18 + 12 + 5 = 35$$
Problem 2 — Exponents vs. Coefficients (Section 2-2)

Find the value of \(3a^2\) and \((3a)^2\) when \(a = 4\).

Solution

In \(3a^2\), only \(a\) is squared; in \((3a)^2\), the entire product \(3a\) is squared.

$$3a^2 = 3 \cdot (4)^2 = 3 \cdot 16 = 48$$ $$(3a)^2 = (3 \cdot 4)^2 = (12)^2 = 144$$

Notice: \((3a)^2 = 144\) is three times as large as \(3a^2 = 48\). The parentheses make a critical difference!

Problem 3 — Solving an Equation (Section 2-3)

Solve \(3r + 2 = 14\), where \(r \in \{3, 4\}\).

Solution

Test each value of \(r\) from the replacement set:

Try \(r = 3\):

\(3(3) + 2 = 9 + 2 = 11\)
\(11 = 14\) ✗ False

Try \(r = 4\):

\(3(4) + 2 = 12 + 2 = 14\)
\(14 = 14\) ✓ True

The solution set is \(\{4\}\).

Problem 4 — Words to Symbols (Section 2-5)

Ken has 4 fewer than one-third as many stamps as Len has. Write an algebraic expression for the number of Ken's stamps.

Solution

Let \(s\) = the number of Len's stamps.

One-third the number of Len's stamps is \(\dfrac{s}{3}\).

"4 fewer than" means subtract 4:

$$\text{Ken's stamps} = \frac{s}{3} - 4$$

Note: \(s\) must be a multiple of 3 greater than or equal to 12 (so that Ken's count is a whole number greater than or equal to 0).

Problem 5 — The Titan Missile (Section 2-6, Four-Step Method)

The main body of the Air Force Titan missile in 1960 was eight times as long as the nose cone. The entire missile was 90 feet in length. How long was the nose cone?

Solution
Step 1. Let \(n\) = the number of feet in the nose cone. Then \(8n\) = the number of feet in the main body. The domain is the numbers of arithmetic (positive numbers). Step 2. Form the equation: $$n + 8n = 90 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 9n = 90$$ Step 3. Solve: the value \(n = 10\) satisfies \(9(10) = 90\). The solution set is \(\{10\}\). Step 4. Check: Nose cone = 10 ft; Main body = \(8 \times 10 = 80\) ft; Total = \(10 + 80 = 90\) ft. ✓

The nose cone was 10 feet long.

Problem 6 — Inequality Word Problem (Section 2-6)

The number of boys on a committee is supposed to be more than three times the number of girls. If 12 boys are put on the committee, what are the possible values for the number of girls?

Solution
Step 1. Let \(x\) = the number of girls, where \(x \in \{\text{whole numbers}\}\). Step 2. "The number of boys is more than three times the number of girls": $$12 > 3x$$ Step 3. Divide both sides by 3: \(4 > x\), so \(x < 4\). Since \(x\) must be a whole number: \(x \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}\). Step 4. Check: \(3 \times 3 = 9\) and \(12 > 9\) ✓. But \(3 \times 4 = 12\) and \(12 > 12\) is false, so 4 is not in the solution set.

The possible number of girls is 0, 1, 2, or 3.

Quick Reference

Vocabulary
Variable — a symbol representing any element of a specified set Replacement set (domain) — the set of permissible values Constant — a variable with exactly one value Term — a product or quotient of numerals/variables Factor — a number being multiplied Coefficient — a factor of a product relative to the remaining factors Base — the expression used as a repeated factor Exponent — tells how many times the base is used as a factor Power — a number expressed using a base and exponent Open sentence — a sentence containing a variable (neither true nor false as written) Equation — a sentence using \(=\) Inequality — a sentence using \(\neq, >, <, \ge, \le\) Solution set — values from the domain making the sentence true Root — a member of the solution set
Summary of Key Ideas
  1. Multiplication with variables is written without a sign: \(3 \times n = 3n\). Parentheses or a raised dot are used between two numerals: \(8 \cdot 7\) or \(8(7)\).
  2. The numerical coefficient of \(ab\) is 1 (since \(ab = 1 \cdot ab\)).
  3. An exponent tells how many times the base is a factor; a coefficient is a factor.
  4. Parentheses determine the base: \(3a^2 \neq (3a)^2\).
  5. To solve an open sentence, find all values from the replacement set that make the sentence true.
  6. The four-step method for word problems: (1) choose a variable, (2) form an equation, (3) solve, (4) check.

The Human Equation: Vieta, Father of Algebra

In the sixteenth century, France and Spain were at war. When the French captured Spanish messengers, they read the encoded messages as easily as any Spaniard could. The Spaniards were so baffled that they complained to the Pope, convinced the French must be using black magic.

In truth, it was a French lawyer named François Viète (Vieta) whose hobby was algebra. He treated code-breaking as nothing more than solving equations. Viète also revolutionized algebra itself: before his time, everything was done in words. He introduced the use of letters as variables—vowels for unknowns, consonants for known numbers—and standardized the use of operation symbols. For these contributions, Viète is known as "the father of algebra."
