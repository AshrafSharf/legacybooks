11-1 · The Nature of Rational Numbers

You define a number system by giving a set of numbers and telling how to add and multiply its members. Starting with the positive integers \(\{1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots\}\), you extend to fractions, then to zero and the negative integers and fractions, arriving at the rational numbers.

Definition — Rational Number

A rational number is any number that can be expressed as the ratio of two integers:

$$\frac{a}{b}, \quad \text{where } a, b \in \mathbb{Z} \text{ and } b \neq 0$$

The system of rational numbers is closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division (except by zero). These are called the rational operations.

A rational number can be expressed in an unlimited number of ways. For example:

$$\frac{5}{6} = \frac{-5}{-6} = \frac{10}{12} = \frac{15}{18} = \frac{20}{24}$$
Key Insight — Density of Rational Numbers

Between every pair of distinct rational numbers there is an infinite set of rational numbers. For instance, a rational number between \(\frac{1}{4}\) and \(\frac{1}{3}\) is their average:

$$\frac{1}{2}\!\left(\frac{1}{4} + \frac{1}{3}\right) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{7}{12} = \frac{7}{24}$$

This process can be repeated without end, so the rationals are dense: between any two, there are infinitely many more.

11-2 · Decimal Forms of Rational Numbers

To change a common fraction to a decimal, carry out the indicated division. Every rational number produces either a terminating or a repeating decimal.

Two Types of Rational Decimals
Terminating Repeating
\(\dfrac{3}{16} = 0.1875\) \(\dfrac{1}{12} = 0.08\overline{3}\)
\(\dfrac{2}{25} = 0.08\) \(\dfrac{7}{11} = 0.\overline{63}\)
\(\dfrac{3}{50} = 0.06\) \(\dfrac{10}{11} = 0.\overline{90}\)

Converting Repeating Decimals to Fractions

Worked Example 1 — Repeating Decimal to Fraction

Write \(0.3\overline{24}\) as a common fraction.

1 Let \(N = 0.32424\ldots\)
2 The repeating block has 2 digits. Multiply by \(10^2 = 100\):
$$100N = 32.424\ldots$$
3 Also: \(N = 0.324\ldots\). To align the repeating parts, multiply by 1:
$$N = 0.324\ldots$$
4 Subtract:
$$99N = 32.1 \implies N = \frac{32.1}{99} = \frac{321}{990} = \frac{107}{330}$$

Answer: \(0.3\overline{24} = \dfrac{107}{330}\)

Key Insight — Rounding Decimals

To round a decimal, add 1 to the last digit kept if the first digit dropped is 5 or more; otherwise, leave the digits unchanged. For example, \(\frac{1}{12} = 0.08\overline{3} \approx 0.083\) (to three decimal places).

11-3 · Roots of Numbers and Irrational Numbers

The operation of raising to a power is called involution. Its inverse operation is called extracting a root (evolution).

Definition — nth Root

For any positive integer \(n\), a number \(x\) is an \(n\)th root of the number \(a\) if it satisfies \(x^n = a\). The expression \(\sqrt[n]{a}\) is called a radical; \(n\) is the root index and \(a\) is the radicand.

With no root index, \(\sqrt{\phantom{x}}\) indicates square root: \(\sqrt{81} = 9\), \(\sqrt{125}\) requires \(\sqrt[3]{125} = 5\).

Every positive number has two square roots — one positive (the principal root, written \(\sqrt{a}\)) and one negative (written \(-\sqrt{a}\)). Together they are written \(\pm\sqrt{a}\).

The Proof That \(\sqrt{2}\) Is Irrational

Theorem — Irrationality of Square Roots of Non-Perfect Squares

If the square root of a positive integer is a rational number \(\frac{a}{b}\) (in lowest terms), then \(b = 1\) and the root is itself an integer. Therefore, only integers which are perfect squares can have rational square roots.

Proof sketch for \(\sqrt{2}\):

  1. Assume \(\sqrt{2} = \frac{a}{b}\) where \(a\) and \(b\) are positive integers with no common factors.
  2. Then \(2 = \frac{a^2}{b^2}\), so \(a^2 = 2b^2\).
  3. Since \(a^2\) is even, \(a\) must be even. Write \(a = 2k\).
  4. Then \(4k^2 = 2b^2\), so \(b^2 = 2k^2\), meaning \(b\) is also even.
  5. But \(a\) and \(b\) both even contradicts our assumption that they share no common factors.

Therefore, \(\sqrt{2}\) cannot be expressed as a ratio of integers — it is irrational.

11-4 · The Real Number Line

Together, the rational and irrational numbers form the real number system. The reals have all the properties you have studied — including density — plus one crucial additional property:

Property — Completeness of the Real Numbers

On the number line, each point corresponds to exactly one real number, and each real number corresponds to exactly one point on the line.

Terminating and repeating decimals represent rational numbers. Decimals that neither terminate nor repeat represent irrational numbers.

11-5 · Geometric Interpretation of Square Roots

How can you locate irrational square roots on the number line without using decimal approximations? The answer comes from the Pythagorean theorem:

Theorem — The Pythagorean Theorem

In any right triangle, the square of the length of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the lengths of the other two sides:

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2$$

Constructing \(\sqrt{2}\) on the Number Line

Draw a square with side length 1. By the Pythagorean theorem, its diagonal has length \(\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{2}\). Using a compass centered at the origin with radius equal to this diagonal, you can mark the point \(\sqrt{2}\) on the number line.

Figure 11-2. Constructing \(\sqrt{2}\) on the number line using the diagonal of a unit square and a compass arc.

By building on this construction — drawing new right triangles with one side equal to a previously constructed root — you can locate \(\sqrt{3}\), \(\sqrt{4}\), \(\sqrt{5}\), and so on:

$$(\sqrt{2})^2 + 1^2 = 3 \implies \sqrt{3}, \qquad (\sqrt{3})^2 + 1^2 = 4 \implies \sqrt{4} = 2, \qquad (\sqrt{4})^2 + 1^2 = 5 \implies \sqrt{5}$$

11-6 · Properties of Square Roots

Two fundamental properties let you work with square roots algebraically. They apply when all radicands are non-negative.

Product Property of Square Roots

If \(a \geq 0\) and \(b \geq 0\), then:

$$\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b}$$
Quotient Property of Square Roots

If \(a \geq 0\) and \(b > 0\), then:

$$\sqrt{\frac{a}{b}} = \frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}$$
Worked Example 2 — Using the Product Property

Evaluate \(\sqrt{2025}\).

1 Factor: \(2025 = 5 \times 405 = 5 \times 5 \times 81 = 5^2 \times 9^2\)
2 Apply the product property:
$$\sqrt{2025} = \sqrt{5^2 \times 9^2} = \sqrt{5^2} \cdot \sqrt{9^2} = 5 \times 9 = 45$$
3 Check: \(45 \times 45 = 2025\) \(\checkmark\)

Answer: \(\sqrt{2025} = 45\)

Worked Example 3 — Using the Quotient Property

Evaluate \(\sqrt{\dfrac{3600}{2401}}\).

1 Factor each:
$$3600 = 6^2 \times 10^2, \qquad 2401 = 7^2 \times 7^2 = 7^4$$
2 Apply the quotient property:
$$\sqrt{\frac{3600}{2401}} = \frac{\sqrt{3600}}{\sqrt{2401}} = \frac{6 \times 10}{7 \times 7} = \frac{60}{49}$$

Answer: \(\sqrt{\dfrac{3600}{2401}} = \dfrac{60}{49}\)

11-7 · Simplifying Radicals

An expression containing a square-root radical is in simplest form when it satisfies three conditions:

Simplest Radical Form — Three Conditions
  1. No integral radicand has a square factor other than 1.
  2. No fractions appear under a radical sign.
  3. No radicals appear in a denominator.

Simplifying radicands with square factors:

$$\sqrt{20} = \sqrt{4 \cdot 5} = 2\sqrt{5}$$ $$\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \cdot 2} = 6\sqrt{2}$$ $$3\sqrt{96} = 3\sqrt{16 \cdot 6} = 3 \cdot 4\sqrt{6} = 12\sqrt{6}$$

Eliminating fractions under a radical:

$$\sqrt{\frac{3}{5}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{5}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{5}}{\sqrt{5}} = \frac{\sqrt{15}}{5}$$

Rationalizing the denominator:

$$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$$ $$\frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3\sqrt{8}} = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{3\sqrt{8}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{2}}{\sqrt{2}} = \frac{2\sqrt{6}}{3\sqrt{16}} = \frac{2\sqrt{6}}{12} = \frac{\sqrt{6}}{6}$$

11-8 · Addition and Subtraction of Radicals

Radicals with the same radicand are called like radicals. You can combine them using the distributive property, just as you combine like terms in algebra:

$$6\sqrt{2} + 5\sqrt{2} = (6 + 5)\sqrt{2} = 11\sqrt{2}$$ $$5\sqrt{6} - 8\sqrt{5} + 2\sqrt{6} + 6\sqrt{5} = 7\sqrt{6} - 2\sqrt{5}$$
Caution — Unlike Radicals

Radicals with different radicands cannot be combined into a single term:

$$\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3} \neq \sqrt{5}$$

The sum can only be indicated, not simplified further.

Worked Example 4 — Combining Radicals

Simplify \(-2\sqrt{48} + 7\sqrt{3} + 2\sqrt{147}\).

1 Simplify each radical:
$$\sqrt{48} = \sqrt{16 \cdot 3} = 4\sqrt{3}, \qquad \sqrt{147} = \sqrt{49 \cdot 3} = 7\sqrt{3}$$
2 Substitute:
$$-2(4\sqrt{3}) + 7\sqrt{3} + 2(7\sqrt{3})$$ $$= -8\sqrt{3} + 7\sqrt{3} + 14\sqrt{3}$$
3 Combine like radicals:
$$= (-8 + 7 + 14)\sqrt{3} = 13\sqrt{3}$$

Answer: \(13\sqrt{3}\)

11-9 · Multiplication of Radicals

The product property lets you multiply radicals directly. Combine the coefficients, combine the radicands, then simplify.

Multiplication Rules
$$\sqrt{a} \cdot \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{ab}$$ $$(m\sqrt{a})(n\sqrt{b}) = mn\sqrt{ab}$$

Always simplify the result to simplest radical form.

Examples of multiplication:

$$\sqrt{7} \cdot \sqrt{3} = \sqrt{21}$$ $$\sqrt{3} \cdot \sqrt{27} = \sqrt{81} = 9$$ $$(2\sqrt{5})(4\sqrt{2}) = 8\sqrt{10}$$

Using the distributive property:

$$\sqrt{x}(\sqrt{x} - 3) = x - 3\sqrt{x}$$ $$2\sqrt{6}(3\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}) = 6\sqrt{12} + 2\sqrt{18} = 12\sqrt{3} + 6\sqrt{2}$$

11-10 · Division of Radicals and Rationalizing Denominators

The process of changing a fraction with an irrational denominator to an equivalent fraction with a rational denominator is called rationalizing the denominator. This makes numerical approximation much easier.

Method — Rationalizing a Single-Term Denominator

Multiply both numerator and denominator by the radical in the denominator:

$$\frac{a}{\sqrt{b}} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{b}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{b}}{\sqrt{b}} = \frac{a\sqrt{b}}{b}$$
Worked Example 5 — Rationalizing a Denominator

Simplify \(\dfrac{6}{\sqrt{3}}\).

1 Multiply numerator and denominator by \(\sqrt{3}\):
$$\frac{6}{\sqrt{3}} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{6\sqrt{3}}{3} = 2\sqrt{3}$$

Answer: \(2\sqrt{3}\)

Worked Example 6 — Simplify and Rationalize

Simplify \(\dfrac{21\sqrt{5}}{7\sqrt{25}}\).

1 Simplify the denominator: \(\sqrt{25} = 5\).
$$\frac{21\sqrt{5}}{7 \cdot 5} = \frac{21\sqrt{5}}{35} = \frac{3\sqrt{5}}{5}$$

Answer: \(\dfrac{3\sqrt{5}}{5}\)

Approximating Irrational Square Roots

The textbook presents a method for finding successive decimal digits of irrational square roots, based on the property of pairs of divisors:

Key Insight — Divisor Pairs

If you divide a number by a divisor smaller than its square root, the quotient will be larger than the square root, and vice versa. So the square root always lies between the divisor and the quotient.

Method — Successive Approximation (Babylonian Method)
  1. Choose a first approximation \(a\) (the integer whose square is nearest to \(n\)).
  2. Divide: compute \(\frac{n}{a}\).
  3. Average: \(\text{new } a = \frac{1}{2}\!\left(a + \frac{n}{a}\right)\).
  4. Repeat steps 2–3 until the desired accuracy is reached.
Practice — Approximate \(\sqrt{17}\)
Step Approximation \(a\) \(17 \div a\) Average
1 \(4\) \(4.25\) \(\frac{4 + 4.25}{2} = 4.125\)
2 \(4.125\) \(4.1212\ldots\) \(\frac{4.125 + 4.1212}{2} \approx 4.1231\)
3 \(4.1231\) \(4.12311\ldots\) \(\approx 4.12311\)

\(\sqrt{17} \approx 4.1231\) (accurate to 4 decimal places).

Quick Reference

The Number System Hierarchy

\(\mathbb{N}\) (Natural) \(\subset\) \(\mathbb{Z}\) (Integers) \(\subset\) \(\mathbb{Q}\) (Rationals) \(\subset\) \(\mathbb{R}\) (Reals)

\(\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{Q} \cup \mathbb{Q}'\), where \(\mathbb{Q}'\) denotes the irrational numbers.

Common Mistakes
  • Writing \(\sqrt{a + b} = \sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}\). This is false! The product property applies to products, not sums.
  • Forgetting that \(\sqrt{a^2} = |a|\), not simply \(a\) (it equals the non-negative value).
  • Leaving a radical in the denominator — always rationalize.
  • Combining unlike radicals: \(3\sqrt{2} + 5\sqrt{3} \neq 8\sqrt{5}\).
  • Claiming negative numbers have real square roots — \(\sqrt{-4}\) is not a real number.
